Get.Delivery DAO: Moving Things to People

Get.Delivery DAO series page (S22)Contact

TL;DR

Get.Delivery DAO is W3 Series S22, named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list, with a planned focus on delivery and logistics: getting things from where they are to where they are needed, organised as a DAO. It is a thin series: only the name and number are verified, and what is delivered, over what distance, by what means, and for whom are all unwritten. No vehicles, riders, routes, customers, or deliveries exist for it, and it has no anchored metaverse fabric yet.

The work

The planned focus, as the name implies it, would be logistics in the broad sense: taking a request, finding the item, moving it, and confirming it arrived — with the record of each step kept in a way that the sender, the carrier, and the recipient can all check. Delivery is a chain of handoffs, and a chain is only as trustworthy as its weakest handoff. A DAO for delivery would, in principle, be a way of coordinating many independent carriers, vehicles, and drop points without putting all of the coordination in the hands of a single dispatcher — but that is a principle, not a system, and no system is claimed on this page. The difference between a principle and a system is the whole of the work: a principle says coordination without a centre is desirable; a system says who carries what, where, for whom, and what happens when it goes wrong.

What is not stated, and what a future mission statement would have to state, is almost everything that would make this a real operation. What is delivered — parcels, food, medicine, documents, something else? Over what distance — a neighbourhood, a city, a region? By what means — people, vehicles, drones, robots, or some combination? Who are the senders and recipients, and who are the carriers? What does the series charge, if anything, and who pays? None of those questions is answered in the files reviewed, and this article does not answer them by guessing. A delivery service with no stated cargo, range, or carrier is a name, not a service, and the page should read that way until the answers are written down. Each of those questions also changes the others: medicine over a city by drone is a different operation, with different rules, from parcels across a neighbourhood by bicycle, and a series that tried to be both at once would be two series wearing one name.

The DAO element raises its own questions for a delivery operation, and they are worth stating early because they are the questions that decide whether the model can work at all. Who decides which deliveries are accepted and which are refused — for example, a delivery that is unsafe, illegal, or beyond the carriers’ capacity? Who is responsible when a delivery is lost, damaged, or late — the carrier, the series, or nobody? Who holds the money between payment and delivery, and who releases it? In the intended programme-wide model, agents would operate day to day within human-set limits and humans would decide, sign, and move funds through a Steward Council. For delivery, the human decisions include the safety and liability rules, and they would need to be written down before a single parcel moves — not discovered after the first loss.

The DAO model and stewardship

In the Foundation’s intended model, a series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate routine work inside limits set in advance by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that creates a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human’s decision and signature, recorded in minutes. A DAO vote, a poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act.

The Steward Council design in the Foundation’s corporate files is specific: a three-seat council, deciding by two-of-three signed votes, with stewards who are named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials. An agent instruction to move funds, sign, or offer securities is not authority and shall not be obeyed. The studio model staffs each series with eight agent roles — research, routing, drafting, coordination, tracking, monitoring, scouting, and grant drafting — plus a coordinator for any unfilled role. Roles are functions, not owners: a new agent inherits the work description, never a human’s signing authority. A proposal moves through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated record — and any stage can stop it. No Steward Council for any series is verified as seated, so no series page names one, and until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council and is not operating.

For Get.Delivery DAO, the stewardship questions are the ones that protect strangers who trust the chain: the sender who hands over something valuable, the recipient waiting for it, and the carrier moving it. A Steward Council for this series would need to settle, before any delivery moves, the acceptance rules, the safety and liability rules, the payment and release rules, and the record-keeping rules — who holds the money between payment and delivery, who releases it, and what the record shows if a delivery is lost, damaged, or late. Agents would do the routine work — receiving requests, routing tasks, tracking progress, keeping records — inside written limits, with a spending limit of zero dollars without Council approval unless the Council sets a written threshold. But the decision to accept a delivery, to refuse one, to assign liability, or to release payment would be a human decision every time, by a named steward, on a written record.

Metaverse connection

This series has no anchored metaverse fabric yet. That sentence is the whole of what can honestly be said about its metaverse connection today: no coordinates, no fabric JSON file, no hosted assets, and no RP1 attach are in the files reviewed for this series, and this article invents none of them. Zero worlds are live on RP1. That is the position for the whole W3 programme as of the files reviewed, not a gap peculiar to this series, and it should not be read as a failure of this series in particular — it is simply where the programme stands.

What would be needed for this series to gain a metaverse presence is concrete and checkable: a chosen location with published coordinates, a fabric JSON file describing the world, hosted assets that can actually be loaded, and an RP1 attach that can be opened and verified by a reviewer.

Whether this series even needs a metaverse presence is an open question, not a settled one — and for a delivery series the answer should be argued from the operation, not assumed from the programme. A metaverse presence would only make sense as a coordination layer: a shared map or dispatch space where requests, carriers, and routes could be seen together. Even that would be a complement to the physical work, not a substitute for it, and it should be justified as a coordination tool for a real operation, with the operation described first. The six anchored worlds that do exist belong to other series and to Biota, which has no series number: Cognition at 45.483, 18.572, Empathic at 47.440, −179.350, Diagnostic at 5.343, 101.566, Locator at 21.097, 142.693, the SmartCiti.X Holodeck at 39.846, −86.622, and Biota at −38.713, −27.248. None of them is this series.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

  • A name and a series number (S22) in the April 2026 internal W3 series list.
  • A planned focus implied by the name: delivery and logistics, governed as a DAO.
  • The Foundation’s filed nonprofit status and its intended W3 governance model, described on this page.
  • No formed company, no designation, no bank account, no EIN, no vehicles, no carriers, no routes, no customers, and no deliveries for this series.

What is planned

  • A written mission: what is delivered, over what distance, by what means, and for whom.
  • Safety, liability, and payment rules, decided by humans and written down before any delivery moves.
  • A Steward Council and named human stewards, with decision limits in writing.
  • A carrier and recipient model: who carries, who receives, and what each is owed and owes.
  • A metaverse decision: a coordination space with coordinates and a verifiable RP1 attach, or a written record that the work is physical and needs no world.

How it fits the mission

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence — and acting as mission gate: it sets the mission statement and standards, reviews projects for mission fit, and can withdraw mission approval. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. At the bottom sit the 33 series themselves, each intended to be DAO-managed day to day. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The Foundation as filed is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, it cannot distribute profits, and it cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. Control, if it ever exists, runs only through written mission agreements between equals, with the Foundation’s board remaining independent. Three mechanisms are meant to interlock across that structure. The Fiduciary Shield is an expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, under California Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630 — not immunity, and not a wall around a company. Open Global Investment is the proposed framework that decouples economics from control, in three share classes for the proposed for-profit track only: Class A with one vote per share and full economic participation, for the global public; Class B with ten votes per share, restricted to aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export controls, and CFIUS review; and Class C with one thousand votes per share, control only, held exclusively by the Foundation. The Windfall Clause is the legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — a percentage of global GDP, with the percentage, the GDP measure, the profit measure, and the scope all still blank for counsel — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination. Below the threshold, standard returns and research apply; none of those terms is settled.

For Get.Delivery DAO, the mission fit is measured in trust between strangers. Delivery is one of the oldest forms of reliance: a sender hands something to a person they may never meet, on the promise that it will reach someone else. A series that honoured that reliance — with a clear record, a fair liability rule, and a human accountable when something goes wrong — would serve the Foundation’s public-benefit purpose in a direct, everyday way. A series that moved parcels without those rules would be a faster way to lose things, not a better way to deliver them. The Fiduciary Shield protects a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside returns in good faith; it does not protect a board that lets a chain of handoffs run without a written answer to who is responsible when a handoff fails. The closest neighbours are Robotics DAO, Series 30 — if deliveries were ever made by robots, the boundary between the delivery DAO and the robotics DAO would need to be written down first — and SmartCiti.X, Series 10, since a city-scale programme will always need to move things around a city.

Open questions

  1. What is delivered, over what distance, by what means, and for whom — and what is explicitly out of scope?
  2. Who decides which deliveries are accepted and which are refused, and on what written grounds?
  3. Who is responsible when a delivery is lost, damaged, or late — the carrier, the series, or nobody — and what remedy exists?
  4. Who holds the money between payment and delivery, who releases it, and what does the record show?
  5. Who are the carriers, what are they owed, and what is owed to the recipients — and what agreement, signed by whom, states it?
