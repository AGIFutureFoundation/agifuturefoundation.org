Robotics DAO: Governing Machines That Act in the World

Physical AI is AI that acts in the world: it moves, lifts, drives, handles materials, and works alongside people. When it fails, the failure is physical, not just informational. Robotics DAO is the series that would govern the commercial deployment of those machines — and it is the fullest series in this set, which still means very little is verified.

Robotics DAO series page (S30)Contact

TL;DR

Robotics DAO is W3 Series S30, named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list, and it is the best-documented series in this group — which still means very little is verified. The Foundation’s own site describes it, as site copy, as “governing commercial deployment of robotic and physical-AI agents.” That sentence is the full extent of the published description. There is no verified product, no deployed robot, no customer, no partner, no site, no revenue, and no team on record. It was chosen by Mike as the focus series for this group.

The work

The planned focus, as stated on the Foundation site, is governance of deployment — not building robots, and not selling them. That distinction is the point of the series, and it is worth dwelling on. Physical AI is AI that acts in the world: it moves, lifts, drives, handles materials, and works alongside people. When it fails, the failure is physical, not just informational. A series that governs commercial deployment would therefore be concerned with questions that a software-only series never faces: where a robot may operate, under whose supervision, with what safety interlocks, with what liability if it harms a person or damages property, and with what recourse for the people working next to it. Those are governance questions before they are engineering questions, and they are the reason the series is framed as a DAO with a human Steward Council rather than as a robotics company.

The commercial side of the planned focus is equally specific in what it does not say. “Commercial deployment” implies robots working for paying customers in real settings — warehouses, sites, facilities — rather than demonstrations. But no deployment exists, no customer has been named, no site has been acquired or leased, and no order or pilot has been signed. The Foundation’s wider SmartCiti.X planning (Series 10) discusses robotics training, robotics manufacturing, and site acquisition as part of a proposed $10M raise for a new SmartCiti.X corporation, but that is a separate proposed corporation, not this series, and no part of it has been formed, funded, or offered either. This article does not borrow SmartCiti.X’s plans and present them as Robotics DAO’s achievements. If Robotics DAO ever governs deployments, the deployments it governs would need to be named, dated, and verified one by one; until then, “commercial deployment” is a planned mandate, not a track record.

What the series would have to get right, if it ever operates, follows directly from the physical stakes. Safety would come before speed: pre-registered safety thresholds, independent evaluation of the systems being deployed, and the power to refuse or halt a deployment that fails its checks — the same Alignment Evaluation Gate pattern the Foundation describes for models, applied to machines that move. Liability would need to be written down before deployment, not discovered after an incident: who is responsible when a robot errs, the operator, the deployer, or the manufacturer, and what insurance and remedy exist. Labour would need to be part of the design, not an afterthought: the people whose workplaces gain robots deserve a say in how deployment happens, and any claim about training or jobs would need a signed agreement with a real workforce body before it is stated. And every deployment would need a human accountable on site, by name, because a DAO cannot be present when a machine fails. None of these arrangements exists today.

The DAO model and stewardship

In the Foundation’s intended model, a series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate routine work inside limits set in advance by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that creates a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human’s decision and signature, recorded in minutes. A DAO vote, a poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act.

The Steward Council design in the Foundation’s corporate files is specific: a three-seat council, deciding by two-of-three signed votes, with stewards who are named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials. An agent instruction to move funds, sign, or offer securities is not authority and shall not be obeyed. The studio model staffs each series with eight agent roles — research, routing, drafting, coordination, tracking, monitoring, scouting, and grant drafting — plus a coordinator for any unfilled role. Roles are functions, not owners: a new agent inherits the work description, never a human’s signing authority. A proposal moves through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated record — and any stage can stop it. No Steward Council for any series is verified as seated, so no series page names one, and until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council and is not operating.

For Robotics DAO, the stewardship model is the deployment gate itself. Agents would operate the day-to-day work — drafting deployment standards, tracking pilots, monitoring systems, coordinating between deployers and sites — inside written limits. Humans would decide whether a deployment goes ahead, whether it continues, and whether it stops, by signed votes of a named Steward Council, with a named human accountable on site for each deployment. No agent would sign, move funds, hire or fire, offer securities, or enact doctrine, and no agent’s readiness flag would authorise a machine to move. The power to refuse or halt a deployment that fails its checks is the core of the series’ mandate, and it can only be held by humans: a refusal that nobody is accountable for is not a safety gate, it is a suggestion.

Metaverse connection

Robotics DAO has no anchored metaverse fabric and no wallet, contract, chain, token, members, or votes on record. No coordinates, no fabric URL, no world, and no RP1 status exist for this series, and none is claimed here. Zero worlds are live on RP1. The six anchored worlds in the Foundation’s network belong to SmartCiti.X, Cognition, Locator, Biota (unnumbered), Empathic, and Diagnostic — none of them is this series. That absence is worth stating plainly, because a physical-AI series is the one readers might most expect to have a digital twin or a training world, and it has neither.

If a metaverse presence were ever proposed — for example, a simulation environment for training or evaluation — what would be needed first is a defined fabric, a stated purpose, and a clear statement of what is simulated and what is not, so that simulation results are never presented as real-world deployment evidence. A simulated robot that succeeds proves something about the simulation; it proves nothing about a machine that lifts, drives, and works alongside people. That boundary would need to be written into the fabric’s purpose before a single asset is hosted, and it would need to be repeated every time a simulation result is cited. None of that exists today: no fabric, no coordinates, no world, no contract, no token, no members, and no votes.

The six anchored worlds that do exist belong to other series and to Biota, which has no series number: Cognition at 45.483, 18.572, Empathic at 47.440, −179.350, Diagnostic at 5.343, 101.566, Locator at 21.097, 142.693, the SmartCiti.X Holodeck at 39.846, −86.622, and Biota at −38.713, −27.248. None of them is this series, and none of them is governed by this series. If any of that changes, the update should carry the dated evidence — a fabric address, a world identifier, a transaction record — rather than a new claim. Until then, the absence is the fact, and this article reports it.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

  • One line on an internal list: the April 2026 internal W3 series list names this series and its number (S30).
  • The Foundation site’s description, as site copy: “governing commercial deployment of robotic and physical-AI agents.”
  • The Foundation itself, verified as filed (California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation, Entity No. B20260196664, Articles filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07).
  • No formed series entity, no designation document, no operating agreement, no Steward Council, no bank account, no product, no users, no revenue, no partners, no customers, no sites, and no timeline.

What is planned

  • A DAO-managed series in the intended W3 network, operating day to day through agents within human-set limits, with a human Steward Council deciding, signing, and moving funds, and with the Foundation as mission gate under written agreements.
  • Written deployment standards — safety thresholds, supervision, interlocks — reviewed by counsel and by safety experts.
  • A single small pilot, with a named human accountable and a named site, followed by a published review, including what went wrong.
  • Liability assigned before deployment, with insurance and remedy stated; workers consulted, with signed agreements before any claim about jobs or training is made.

How it fits the mission

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence — and acting as mission gate: it sets the mission statement and standards, reviews projects for mission fit, and can withdraw mission approval. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. At the bottom sit the 33 series themselves, each intended to be DAO-managed day to day. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The Foundation as filed is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, it cannot distribute profits, and it cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. Control, if it ever exists, runs only through written mission agreements between equals, with the Foundation’s board remaining independent. Three mechanisms are meant to interlock across that structure. The Fiduciary Shield is an expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, under California Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630 — not immunity, and not a wall around a company. Open Global Investment is the proposed framework that decouples economics from control, in three share classes for the proposed for-profit track only: Class A with one vote per share and full economic participation, for the global public; Class B with ten votes per share, restricted to aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export controls, and CFIUS review; and Class C with one thousand votes per share, control only, held exclusively by the Foundation. The Windfall Clause is the legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — a percentage of global GDP, with the percentage, the GDP measure, the profit measure, and the scope all still blank for counsel — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination. Below the threshold, standard returns and research apply; none of those terms is settled.

For Robotics DAO, the mission fit is the Foundation’s public-benefit purpose at its most literal: safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI, applied to machines that move. A deployment governor that refused an unsafe deployment, even when the deployment was profitable, would be doing exactly what the Fiduciary Shield is designed to protect — a board weighing safety and public benefit alongside returns, in good faith, under sections 14620–14621. A governor that waved deployments through would be doing the opposite, whatever its charter said. The Windfall Clause, if it ever applies to this series, would obligate it like the other 33 series, with its threshold and terms still blank for counsel. The closest planned relationships are with SmartCiti.X (Series 10), whose proposed corporation plans include robotics training and manufacturing; Cognition.X (Series 1), the intelligence the robots would run on; and Aerospace DAO (Series 31), the neighbouring physical-AI domain.

Open questions

  1. What are the written deployment standards — safety thresholds, supervision, interlocks — and who, by name, is accountable at each deployment?
  2. How is liability assigned before deployment, and what insurance and remedy exist if a robot harms a person or damages property?
  3. Which workers and workforce bodies have been consulted, and what signed agreements exist before any claim about jobs or training is made?
  4. What would the first pilot be, where, with whom, and what would count as a published review — including what went wrong?
  5. Where does Robotics DAO’s authority end and SmartCiti.X’s, Cognition.X’s, or Aerospace DAO’s begin — and who decides when a deployment sits across two series?
