The work

Curable.X is Series 4 in the April 2026 internal W3 series list. That sentence, together with the name itself, is very nearly everything verified that can be said about this series. Its name implies a focus on cures and curative treatment — on making people better, not only on diagnosing what is wrong with them — and this article describes that as a planned focus, because no product, condition, therapy, research programme, partner, clinician, trial, or publication for Curable.X is verified in the files. This is a thin article on purpose: where there is almost nothing verified to say, the honest article says so plainly instead of filling the space with invention.

The domain the name implies is worth sketching carefully, because the sketch itself shows how much is still undefined. “Curable” could point at many different kinds of work: developing a treatment for a specific condition, improving how existing treatments are chosen or delivered, supporting recovery and rehabilitation, or building tools that help clinicians and patients track whether a treatment is working. Each of those is a different venture, with different expertise, different regulation, and different risks, and no verified document says which of them Curable.X intends. The name alone does not choose between them, and this article does not choose on the series’ behalf.

What can be said without inventing anything is what a curative series would have to get right if it were built. A treatment claim is a claim about a human body, so it would need evidence: what condition, what intervention, what outcome, measured how, in whom, and with what risks. It would need to be clear about the line between supporting treatment decisions and making them, and about who — a named human clinician, not an agent — bears responsibility for a decision about a patient. It would need to say what it does not do, as clearly as what it does. None of those statements exists for Curable.X today, and their absence is not a gap to be papered over with confident language; it is the current state of the series, and it is stated here as such.

There is one neighbouring fact that needs to be handled explicitly, because it could easily confuse a reader. The internal list also contains a Series 17 named Curable.Blockchain DAO. No verified document explains the relationship between Curable.X (Series 4) and Curable.Blockchain DAO (Series 17) — whether they are two views of one effort, two separate efforts in the same domain, or a naming overlap that was never resolved. This article does not merge them, does not assign Series 17’s implied blockchain focus to this series, and does not claim any shared work between them. If the relationship is ever defined in writing, this article should be updated to say what it is and cite the document. Until then, the two names stand side by side in the list, unexplained.

The most useful next step for Curable.X would be the smallest one that is still checkable: a written statement of scope from a named human steward, naming the first condition or the first kind of curative work, and the first thing the series would refuse to do. A series that cannot name its first condition cannot be judged on anything else, and a series that will not name what it refuses to do has not yet understood the domain it claims. Until that statement exists, the honest size of Curable.X is a name, a number, and a direction — and this article leaves it at that.

Metaverse connection

Curable.X has no anchored metaverse fabric yet. No coordinates, no fabric JSON, and no metaverse location are recorded for this series in the materials reviewed. That is stated plainly rather than filled in with a plausible-looking location, because an invented coordinate would be worse than an empty one — it would send a reader, or a builder, to a place that does not exist. Zero worlds are live on RP1.

What would be needed for a metaverse presence is the same sequence that the anchored series went through, and it has not started for Curable.X: a decision about what a world for this series would be for, a fabric definition published at a checkable address, the assets that fabric references hosted at the paths it expects, and a verified render in Artemis. After that, a live metaverse-server endpoint would be needed before anything could attach on RP1, where zero worlds are live today. None of those steps has been taken, and none is claimed here.

It is worth asking whether a curative series needs a metaverse world at all, and the honest answer is that it probably does not need one first. A treatment-focused series’ first home is a clinic, a laboratory, or a trial protocol — not a world. A world could one day host training or patient education, but only after the series could say what it treats and how it knows its treatment works. Building the world first would put the venue ahead of the evidence, and this article does not recommend that order.

The DAO model and stewardship

The operating model planned for every W3 series is the same, and it is worth stating in full on each article page, because it is the part of the plan that decides who can actually do anything. In the intended model, Curable.X would be DAO-managed day to day: agents operate the routine work within limits set by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that carries a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare; humans execute. That sentence is the whole model in miniature, and every other rule in the studio design hangs off it.

The studio model names eight agent roles for each series: research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting, with a coordinator covering any role left unfilled. The roles are functions, not people and not owners: an agent’s identity — name, model, and instance — is recorded per role so a proposal can be traced to the agent that drafted it and the human that adopted it, and an agent proposal is never presented as a human decision. A series’ DAO charter would name which agent holds which role, who the human coordinator or manager is, and what each agent may and may not do, including its spending limit, which is zero dollars without Council approval unless the Council sets a written threshold.

The human gate is where the model gets its teeth. The Steward Council is the human decision-making body for the series: three named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials, deciding by 2-of-3 signed votes, minuted, on budgets, major contracts, hiring, fundraising, and charter changes. A DAO vote, a token vote, a chat poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act: it does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities. Only the council, with signed authority, does that. Agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charter changes.

Proposals would move through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review by the Foundation, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated receipt — and through six stage gates, from idea to exit or wind-down, with a human decision at each gate. Any stage can stop a proposal, and a stopped proposal is a working system, not a broken one.

None of this is operating for Curable.X today. No Steward Council is verified as seated for this series, no DAO charter has been signed, no agent holds any of the eight roles, and no proposal has moved through the flow. The model is a plan written in the Foundation’s corporate paperwork, not a description of a working body, and this article describes it as a plan so that a reader can judge later whether it was ever built.

How it fits the mission

Curable.X sits in the third tier of the Foundation’s intended three-tier architecture. The apex tier is governance: the AGI Future Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the mission standard — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — and acting as mission gate for the series below it. The middle tier is the commercial engine: W3 LLC, an intended Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. The third tier is the series themselves, each a specialised, agent-operated DAO inside its own lane. Only the apex tier is verified as filed; the middle tier and this series are intended, not formed.

Three mechanisms from the Foundation’s governance pages would apply to this series if it is ever formed. The Fiduciary Shield is the proposed protection for a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside returns: California benefit-corporation status expands the business-judgment rule for benefit-considering decisions, under Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630. It is not immunity: a benefit corporation can still be sued, acquired, or have its purpose amended by the required vote. Open Global Investment is the proposed model that decouples economics from control — broad economic participation through Class A shares with one vote each, a larger voice for screened partners through Class B with ten votes, and decisive control through Class C with one thousand votes, held only by the Foundation — but it belongs to the proposed for-profit benefit-corporation track, not to the filed nonprofit, and counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. The Windfall Clause is a planned legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — to be set by counsel, expressed in the pitch framework as a percentage of global GDP — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination; the threshold, share, recipient, and enforceability are all blank for counsel today.
