InfoGurus: Knowledge Organisation, Expertise, and Answers You Can Check

InfoGurus is W3 Series 33, the last name on the April 2026 internal W3 series list, and its name implies an information lane: expertise, guidance, and answers. The honest thing to say first is that its boundaries have not been set. This article explores what an information series would have to get right — accuracy, provenance, and the line between information and advice — and why choosing one lane matters more than claiming all of them.

Status — please read first: Named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list. W3 LLC formation and per-series designation are not verified in filings — this series is intended/planned, not formed, funded, or offered.

TL;DR

InfoGurus (S33) is the last series on the April 2026 internal W3 list, planned as an information and expertise series with no verified scope, product, user, or partner. Its name is broad enough to mean several different ventures — a reference service, an expert network, a research function for the network, or a public information service — and no document chooses between them. It has no anchored metaverse fabric yet, and zero worlds are live on RP1. The series is intended and planned, not formed, funded, or offered.

The work

InfoGurus is W3 Series 33, the last series on the April 2026 internal W3 series list. The list names “W3 LLC-InfoGurus — Series 33” and gives no further description, so the scope on this page is a planned focus drawn from what the name implies, labelled as planned throughout. The name implies an information lane: expertise, guidance, and answers — people, or agents, that know things and help others find and use what they know. That is a broad implication, and the honest thing to say is that its boundaries have not been set.

The breadth of the name is the first problem this series would have to solve, and this article states it rather than smoothing it over. “Information” could mean a reference or knowledge service, an expert network, a research and synthesis function for the rest of the W3 network, or a public information service. Those are different products with different users, different quality standards, and different risks, and a series that tried to be all of them would be none of them well. The first real decision for InfoGurus — before any build — would be to write down, in one page, which of those it is and which it is not, and to have a human Steward Council approve that scope. Until that page exists, InfoGurus is a name awaiting a definition, and this article will keep saying so.

What can be said with confidence is what the series would have to get right in any of those lanes, because information work carries its own specific risks. Accuracy would be the first standard: an information service that states things it has not verified misleads exactly the people who trusted it most, and a service that presents opinion as fact, or a single source as consensus, fails the Foundation’s standard before it starts. Provenance would be the second: every substantive claim should carry its source and its date, so a reader can check it rather than take the service’s word. And the boundary around advice would be the third: general information is one thing, and professional advice — legal, medical, financial — is another, requiring licensed humans, disclaimers that are actually read, and escalation to a qualified person rather than an answer from a model. None of these are policies in force; they are the standards the series would be expected to meet, listed here so a future reviewer can check whether they were met.

There is also a plausible role for InfoGurus inside the network itself, and it is worth naming as a possibility rather than a plan: a shared research and synthesis function that helps the other thirty-two series find, check, and use information — a service to the network rather than a product for the public. That role would make InfoGurus the most inward-facing series in the group, and it would be judged by a different standard: not by users or volume, but by whether the other series trust its work enough to rely on it. Whether that is the right role, or whether InfoGurus should face outward to the public, is an open question that would need a human decision, not an assumption. No service to the network, and no public product, exists today.

Validation for InfoGurus would be measured in trust and in correction, not in volume. An information service earns trust by being right, by citing its sources, and — most importantly — by correcting itself visibly when it is wrong. The planned sequence is therefore: written scope first, choosing one lane and naming the lanes it excludes; then a small, testable service, with sources and dates on every claim and a published corrections log; then review by the people it serves, including whether they found it accurate and useful; and only then any discussion of scale. A service that grows before it has published its first correction has not yet learned the habit that makes information work trustworthy. Until that habit is demonstrated, InfoGurus remains a planned focus, and this article keeps it described that way.

Metaverse connection

InfoGurus has no anchored metaverse fabric yet. No coordinates, no fabric URL, no world, and no RP1 status exist for this series, and none is claimed here. Zero worlds are live on RP1. The six anchored worlds in the Foundation’s network belong to SmartCiti.X, Cognition.X, Locator.X, Biota.blockchain (unnumbered), Empathic.X, and Diagnostic.X — none of them is this series. An information service could one day have a presence in-world — for example, a place where people ask questions and check answers — but a world is a venue, not a source, and in-world answers would need the same sourcing and dating as answers anywhere else. What would be needed first is a defined fabric and a stated purpose; neither exists today.

That is the complete, verified statement of this series’ metaverse position. There is nothing further to add, because there is nothing further on record: no fabric, no coordinates, no world, no contract, no token, no members, and no votes. If any of that changes, this article should be updated with the dated evidence — a fabric address, a world identifier, a transaction record — rather than with a new claim. Until then, the absence is the fact, and this article reports it.

It is worth adding why the absence matters less here than it might for other series. An information service’s first home is a page, a document, or a conversation — not a world. A metaverse presence would be a distribution choice made after the service had proved it could answer accurately and correct itself visibly. Building the world first would put the venue ahead of the thing the venue exists to carry, and this article does not recommend that order.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

One line on an internal list: the April 2026 W3 series list names this series and its number. The Foundation itself is verified as filed. That is the complete inventory. There is no formed series entity, no designation document, no operating agreement, no Steward Council, no bank account, no product, no users, no revenue, no partners, no customers, no sites, and no timeline. This article does not present a plan as an achievement, and it does not fill the empty inventory with plausible-sounding detail.

What is planned

A DAO-managed series in the intended W3 network, operating day to day through agents within human-set limits, with a human Steward Council deciding, signing, and moving funds, and with the Foundation as mission gate under written agreements. Before any of that can happen, the series needs a written statement of purpose, a designated legal form, counsel’s review, a named steward, and a first stage-gate decision. Each of those is a human decision with a dated record. None has been made yet.

The DAO model and stewardship

The operating model planned for every W3 series is the same, and it is worth stating in full on each article page, because it is the part of the plan that decides who can actually do anything. In the intended model, InfoGurus would be DAO-managed day to day: agents operate the routine work within limits set by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that carries a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare; humans execute. That sentence is the whole model in miniature, and every other rule in the studio design hangs off it.

The studio model names eight agent roles for each series: research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting, with a coordinator covering any role left unfilled. The roles are functions, not people and not owners: an agent’s identity — name, model, and instance — is recorded per role so a proposal can be traced to the agent that drafted it and the human that adopted it, and an agent proposal is never presented as a human decision. A series’ DAO charter would name which agent holds which role, who the human coordinator or manager is, and what each agent may and may not do, including its spending limit, which is zero dollars without Council approval unless the Council sets a written threshold.

The human gate is where the model gets its teeth. The Steward Council is the human decision-making body for the series: three named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials, deciding by 2-of-3 signed votes, minuted, on budgets, major contracts, hiring, fundraising, and charter changes. A DAO vote, a token vote, a chat poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act: it does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities. Only the council, with signed authority, does that. Agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charter changes.

Proposals would move through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review by the Foundation, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated receipt — and through six stage gates, from idea to exit or wind-down, with a human decision at each gate. Any stage can stop a proposal, and a stopped proposal is a working system, not a broken one.

None of this is operating for InfoGurus today. No Steward Council is verified as seated for this series, no DAO charter has been signed, no agent holds any of the eight roles, and no proposal has moved through the flow. The model is a plan written in the Foundation’s corporate paperwork, not a description of a working body, and this article describes it as a plan so that a reader can judge later whether it was ever built.

How it fits the mission

InfoGurus sits in the third tier of the Foundation’s intended three-tier architecture. The apex tier is governance: the AGI Future Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the mission standard — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — and acting as mission gate for the series below it. The middle tier is the commercial engine: W3 LLC, an intended Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. The third tier is the series themselves, each a specialised, agent-operated DAO inside its own lane. Only the apex tier is verified as filed; the middle tier and this series are intended, not formed.

Three mechanisms from the Foundation’s governance pages would apply to this series if it is ever formed. The Fiduciary Shield is the proposed protection for a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside returns: California benefit-corporation status expands the business-judgment rule for benefit-considering decisions, under Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630. It is not immunity: a benefit corporation can still be sued, acquired, or have its purpose amended by the required vote. Open Global Investment is the proposed model that decouples economics from control — broad economic participation through Class A shares with one vote each, a larger voice for screened partners through Class B with ten votes, and decisive control through Class C with one thousand votes, held only by the Foundation — but it belongs to the proposed for-profit benefit-corporation track, not to the filed nonprofit, and counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. The Windfall Clause is a planned legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — to be set by counsel, expressed in the pitch framework as a percentage of global GDP — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination; the threshold, share, recipient, and enforceability are all blank for counsel today.
