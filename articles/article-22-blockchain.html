Blockchain

Blockchain is Series 6 in the April 2026 internal W3 series list: a planned domain whose name points at ledger, record-keeping, and decentralised coordination infrastructure for the W3 network. Almost nothing is verified about it beyond the name and number, and this article says so plainly while going deeper on why a ledger layer matters to the whole architecture, what it would have to decide before it could be built, and where its boundaries lie.

TL;DR

Blockchain is W3 Series S06, named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list, and almost nothing is verified about it beyond the name and number. Its planned domain is ledger and coordination infrastructure, which could serve the other series as shared record-keeping for votes, provenance, and decisions, or could become a venture in its own right, and no verified document says which. No chain, protocol, token, smart contract, application, partner, or publication is verified for this series. It is intended and planned, not formed, funded, or offered, and it has no anchored metaverse fabric yet.

The work

The domain the name implies is worth sketching carefully, because the sketch itself shows how much is still undefined. A series called simply “Blockchain” names a technology, not a field of application, and a technology without a stated application is a tool waiting for a job. Unlike Cognition.X or Diagnostic.X, which name a field, this series names the ledger itself. That breadth is itself information: it leaves open whether the series is infrastructure for the other series, a venture building blockchain applications for outside users, or both, and no verified document chooses among those. This article does not choose on the series’ behalf. What it does is lay out what each reading would demand, so that a future steward can see the decisions waiting inside the name.

The infrastructure reading is the more natural one inside this family. Thirty-three intended series, each DAO-managed day-to-day with a human Steward Council deciding, signing, and moving funds, will generate decisions that need to be recorded, checked, and revisited: proposals, votes, budgets, mission-fit reviews, and the records a Fiduciary Shield defence would depend on. A shared ledger could make those records auditable, so that a decision claimed months later can be checked against the record made at the time. That is the strongest case for a Blockchain series: not a product for the public, but a spine for the network, holding the evidence that governance actually happened. Even on that reading, the hard questions arrive immediately. What is recorded on-chain and what is deliberately kept off it? Who can read and write each kind of record? What happens when a record is wrong and needs correcting, and who has the authority to correct it? A ledger that cannot be corrected is a liability; a ledger that can be rewritten at will is not a ledger. Those are design decisions for a named human steward and counsel, not details a landing page can settle.

The venture reading raises a different set of questions, and they are sharper. A series building blockchain applications for outside users would need to say which chain or chains it uses and why, what its applications actually do, who its users are, and how it earns anything at all. Above all it would need to be clear about tokens: whether any token exists, what it does and does not confer, and whether it is a security. Those questions need counsel’s written advice, not a landing page’s assurance, and no token, protocol, or application is verified for this series today. The discipline here is to keep the token question open and unanswered rather than to let the word “blockchain” smuggle in an assumption that a token exists, that one will exist, or that one should.

There is one neighbouring fact that needs to be handled explicitly, because it could easily confuse a reader. The internal list also contains three other blockchain-named series: Curable.Blockchain DAO (Series 17), BioDAO.blockchain DAO (Series 18), and Genome.Blockchain (Series 28). No verified document explains how Blockchain (Series 6) relates to any of them, whether it is intended as shared infrastructure beneath them, a separate effort that happens to share a technology, or a naming overlap that was never resolved. This article does not merge them, does not assign their domains to this series, and does not claim any shared work between them. If the relationship is ever defined in writing, the record should be updated to say what it is and cite the document. Until then the four names stand side by side in the list, unexplained, and the general Blockchain series remains the least defined of the four. That is not a flaw in this article; it is the accurate state of the record.

Finally, the governance angle deserves emphasis, because it is where a blockchain series either earns its place or becomes a slogan. A DAO that cannot be audited or challenged is a slogan, not a governance system. If Blockchain ever serves the network, its value will be measured by whether stewards, reviewers, and the public can check what was decided, when, by whom, and on what evidence, and whether a decision can be reversed when it proves wrong. That standard applies to the series itself as well: its own scope, its own chain choice, its own token analysis, and its own relationship to the other blockchain-named series would each need to be written down, approved by humans, and published before they could be claimed. Until then, the honest public record is a name, a number, a planned domain, and a list of open questions.

The DAO model and stewardship

The planned governance model for Blockchain is the model described across the Foundation's governance and operations pages, and it is worth stating in full here because it is the part of the plan that most often gets misread. Day-to-day work would be agent-managed: agents would operate routine work within limits set in advance by humans, in the eight roles of the Venture Studio Operating Model (research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting). Those roles describe functions, not people and not owners, and no agent holds any of them for Blockchain today.

Above the agents sits the human gate. A human Steward Council would decide, sign, and move funds: a DAO vote, a token vote, or an agent's readiness flag is not a corporate act, and only a named human acting within signed, written authority can sign a contract, move money, issue securities, or enact a charter. The Council would set the limits inside which agents may act, review what agents did, and remain accountable.

The hard limits bind every agent role equally. Agents NEVER sign, move funds, hire or fire, offer securities, or enact doctrine or charters. Agents prepare; humans execute. For Blockchain, that model is planned, not operating: no Steward Council is verified as seated, no operating agreement is in the files reviewed, and no published set of agent limits exists.

Metaverse connection

There is no anchored metaverse fabric yet for Blockchain: no coordinates, no fabric JSON file, no hosted assets, and no RP1 attach are verified for it, and this article invents none of them. That is stated plainly rather than filled in with a plausible-looking location, because an invented coordinate would be worse than an empty one.

Zero worlds are live on RP1, for the whole W3 programme as of the files reviewed, not a gap peculiar to Blockchain. A metaverse presence would need four checkable things: published coordinates, a fabric JSON file, hosted assets that can actually be loaded, and an RP1 attach a reviewer can open and verify. Until all four exist, the correct statement remains no anchored metaverse fabric yet. Whether this item even needs one is an open question: either anchor a fabric because the work is better done in a shared spatial place, or record that the work happens in documents, code, and human conversation and needs no world.

How it fits the mission

Blockchain fits the mission as infrastructure for accountability, not as a product with customers. The Foundation's architecture depends on records: a board that wants the Fiduciary Shield's expanded business-judgment protection must be able to show it considered the benefit purpose in good faith, and a Windfall Clause pre-commitment is only credible if the profits, the threshold, and the distribution can be checked. A ledger series, if it is ever built as shared infrastructure, would be the layer that makes those claims checkable. That is a supporting role, and it should be described as one: Blockchain does not carry the mission by itself, but the mission's promises are harder to trust without a record-keeping layer that works.

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the AGI Future Foundation, a filed California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation (Entity No. B20260196664, Articles filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07), as governance and mission steward. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC, and below it the 33 named W3 sub-series in the April 2026 internal list. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The middle and bottom tiers are intended, not established, and nothing on this page should be read as saying the Foundation owns, controls, or has formed Blockchain.

Three mechanisms hold the intended structure together, and each is a plan with work still to do. The Fiduciary Shield is expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions under California Corporations Code sections 14620 to 14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630. It is not immunity: a board can still be sued and must still act in good faith. Open Global Investment is the proposed for-profit benefit-corporation framework only: Class A, one vote and full economics, open to the global public; Class B, ten votes, for restricted aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export-control, and foreign-investment rules; Class C, 1,000 votes, control-only, reserved to the Foundation. Its suggested first tranche is a SAFE of $500,000 to $1,500,000 with a suggested cap of $8 million to $15 million, illustrative only, and a second priced tranche whose price would be set then, not today. The PBC track and the AGI Corp SAFE are alternative issuers, never both for the same round, and nothing has been issued under either. The Windfall Clause is a pre-commitment, hardwired before profits exist, that profits above a threshold set as a percentage of global GDP would be shared with humanity through four Benefit Buckets, Global Public Goods, Healthcare Access, Energy Infrastructure, and Technological Self-Determination, for the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Its threshold measure, scope, share, and recipient are blank for counsel, and it can be amended only by supermajority plus Class C.

Two cautions belong in the same passage. First, the filed Foundation is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head, and its board must remain independent, with control only through written mission agreements designed with counsel. The for-profit track is separate and proposed, and counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. Second, the Foundation's fundraising as filed is donation and grant only. No suggested figure here is an offering, a price, a term, or a promise of any return, and suggested figures are never added into a family total.
