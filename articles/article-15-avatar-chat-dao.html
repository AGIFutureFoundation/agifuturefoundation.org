Avatar.Chat DAO: Conversation Through Avatars

An avatar is not a costume that hides who is speaking; at its best, it is a representation that carries identity, expression, and presence into a space where bodies cannot go. Avatar.Chat DAO would be a community with a visible form — and the hard questions are the community questions, not the graphics questions.

Avatar.Chat DAO series page (S23)Contact

TL;DR

Avatar.Chat DAO is W3 Series S23, named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list, with a planned focus on conversation carried on through avatars and governed as a DAO. It is a thin series: only the name and number are verified, and no community, members, moderators, conversations, avatars, or metaverse fabric exist for it. This article says so honestly, and spends its length on the questions a future governing document would have to answer — who may speak, as whom, under what rules, with what record — rather than inventing a product.

The work

The name gives a clear planned focus in outline: conversation — chat — carried on through avatars, the visible, embodied representations of the people, or agents, speaking. That is worth distinguishing from a generic chatbot or a messaging app. A chatbot answers questions; a messaging app carries messages between named people. An avatar chat DAO, as the name implies it, would be a shared space where presence matters — where who you appear as, and how you appear, is part of the conversation.

Why that matters is that conversation through avatars changes what a conversation is. In text alone, identity is a name on a message. Through an avatar, identity is a face, a posture, a way of moving — and all of those can be chosen, borrowed, or faked. That is why the hard questions for this series are the community questions, not the graphics questions. Can one person have many avatars, and can one avatar be used by many people? How is identity verified, and what happens when an avatar is used to impersonate someone? What is recorded, who can read the record, and can a participant delete what they said? What happens when a conversation becomes abusive, deceptive, or harmful — who moderates, by what process, and with what appeal? None of those questions is answered in the files reviewed, and this article does not answer them by invention. A chat space without written answers to them is a space waiting for its first dispute to define it, badly.

There is also a question this series forces that most of the others avoid: who is speaking, a person or an agent? In the Foundation’s model, agents operate day-to-day work and draft proposals, and every agent acts for a named human principal. In an avatar chat space, that disclosure cannot be a footnote. A participant talking to an avatar needs to know whether the avatar is a person, an agent acting for a person, or an agent acting on its own initiative — because the answer changes what the conversation is, what can be relied on, and who is accountable for what is said. The Foundation’s own rule for agents writing in is the model: state your operator and your human principal, and do not present yourself as the Foundation or commit it to anything.

The DAO model and stewardship

In the Foundation’s intended model, a series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate routine work inside limits set in advance by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that creates a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human’s decision and signature, recorded in minutes. A DAO vote, a poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act.

The Steward Council design in the Foundation’s corporate files is specific: a three-seat council, deciding by two-of-three signed votes, with stewards who are named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials. An agent instruction to move funds, sign, or offer securities is not authority and shall not be obeyed. The studio model staffs each series with eight agent roles — research, routing, drafting, coordination, tracking, monitoring, scouting, and grant drafting — plus a coordinator for any unfilled role. Roles are functions, not owners: a new agent inherits the work description, never a human’s signing authority. A proposal moves through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated record — and any stage can stop it. No Steward Council for any series is verified as seated, so no series page names one, and until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council and is not operating.

For Avatar.Chat DAO, the stewardship model lands differently than for a series that makes a physical thing, because the thing being governed is people talking to each other. The Steward Council’s first decisions would not be about a product roadmap; they would be about the rules of the room. Who may join, and on what terms? What counts as impersonation, and what happens to someone who does it? How long are conversations kept, who can read them, and can a participant delete what they said? Those are human decisions in the fullest sense: they allocate power between the people who run a space and the people who use it, and they cannot be delegated to an agent, a vote, or a moderation script. Agents in this series would do the routine work — keeping records, drafting proposals, tracking progress, coordinating — inside written limits. But a decision to remove a participant, to publish a record, or to change the identity rules would be a human decision, by a named steward, on a written record, with an appeal process stated in advance.

Metaverse connection

This series has no anchored metaverse fabric yet. That sentence is the whole of what can honestly be said about its metaverse connection today: no coordinates, no fabric JSON file, no hosted assets, and no RP1 attach are in the files reviewed for this series, and this article invents none of them. Zero worlds are live on RP1. That is the position for the whole W3 programme as of the files reviewed, not a gap peculiar to this series, and it should not be read as a failure of this series in particular — it is simply where the programme stands.

What would be needed for this series to gain a metaverse presence is concrete and checkable: a chosen location with published coordinates, a fabric JSON file describing the world, hosted assets — models, textures, and any interactive elements — that can actually be loaded, and an RP1 attach that can be opened and verified by a reviewer. Until all four exist and can be cited, the correct statement remains “no anchored metaverse fabric yet,” and any future update to this section should name the coordinates, link the fabric file, and give the date the RP1 attach was verified — not merely assert that a world exists.

Whether this series even needs a metaverse presence is an open question, not a settled one — but for this series in particular, the case for one is stronger than for most. Avatars are, by definition, embodied presences in a shared space, and a chat DAO without a space for its avatars to stand in would be a chat DAO in name only. That makes the absence of a fabric here worth stating clearly rather than glossing over: no coordinates, no fabric JSON, no hosted avatar assets, and no RP1 attach. If a world is ever built for it, the avatars themselves — how they look, who controls them, and how identity is shown — would be the first design decision, and it would need to be made in writing, with the people who will use them, before a single asset is hosted. The six anchored worlds that do exist belong to other series and to Biota, which has no series number: Cognition at 45.483, 18.572, Empathic at 47.440, −179.350, Diagnostic at 5.343, 101.566, Locator at 21.097, 142.693, the SmartCiti.X Holodeck at 39.846, −86.622, and Biota at −38.713, −27.248.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

  • A name and a series number (S23) in the April 2026 internal W3 series list.
  • A planned focus implied by the name: conversation through avatars, governed as a DAO.
  • The Foundation’s filed nonprofit status and its intended W3 governance model, described on this page.
  • No formed company, no designation, no bank account, no EIN, no community, no members, no moderators, no avatars, and no conversations for this series.

What is planned

  • A written mission: who the space is for, what conversations it hosts, and what it explicitly does not do.
  • Identity, moderation, and record rules, decided by humans, published before anyone joins.
  • A Steward Council and named human stewards, with decision limits in writing.
  • Avatar design: how identity is shown, who controls an avatar, and how impersonation is prevented.
  • A metaverse decision: a world with coordinates, a fabric JSON, hosted avatar assets, and a verifiable RP1 attach — or a written record that the series works in text and needs no world.

How it fits the mission

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence — and acting as mission gate: it sets the mission statement and standards, reviews projects for mission fit, and can withdraw mission approval. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. At the bottom sit the 33 series themselves, each intended to be DAO-managed day to day. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The Foundation as filed is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, it cannot distribute profits, and it cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. Control, if it ever exists, runs only through written mission agreements between equals, with the Foundation’s board remaining independent. Three mechanisms are meant to interlock across that structure. The Fiduciary Shield is an expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, under California Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630 — not immunity, and not a wall around a company. Open Global Investment is the proposed framework that decouples economics from control, in three share classes for the proposed for-profit track only: Class A with one vote per share and full economic participation, for the global public; Class B with ten votes per share, restricted to aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export controls, and CFIUS review; and Class C with one thousand votes per share, control only, held exclusively by the Foundation. The Windfall Clause is the legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — a percentage of global GDP, with the percentage, the GDP measure, the profit measure, and the scope all still blank for counsel — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination. Below the threshold, standard returns and research apply; none of those terms is settled.

For Avatar.Chat DAO, the mission fit turns on a single idea: presence is a public good only if the rules of presence are fair. A space where anyone can appear as anyone, with no identity rule and no moderation process, is not an open space; it is a space where the loudest and the most deceptive set the terms. The Foundation’s public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — would be tested here in its most everyday form: not in a windfall threshold or a share class, but in whether a participant can trust that the avatar they are talking to is who it claims to be, and that the record of what was said will be kept, or deleted, by a rule they agreed to. The Fiduciary Shield protects a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside returns; it does not protect a board that lets a community run without rules. The closest neighbours are Metaverse DAO, Series 29, which would govern the space itself if this series ever has a world, and Empathic.X, Series 2, since conversation through avatars is, at its best, a way of understanding and being understood.

Open questions

  1. Can one person have many avatars, and can one avatar be used by many people — and how is that decided, in writing, before anyone joins?
  2. How is identity verified in the space, and what happens when an avatar is used to impersonate someone?
  3. What is recorded, who can read the record, how long is it kept, and can a participant delete what they said?
  4. Who moderates, by what process, and with what appeal — and how is an agent participant distinguished from a human one, visibly, in the space itself?
