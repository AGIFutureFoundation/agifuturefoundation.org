Metaverse DAO: The Governance Layer for Shared Worlds

Metaverse DAO is not a world. It is not a place you visit, and it is not the owner of the six anchored worlds. It is the planned governance layer for the metaverse itself — the series that would decide how worlds are run, who can build in them, what agents may do there, and how the worlds relate to the series that anchor them.

TL;DR

Metaverse DAO is W3 Series S29, named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list, with a planned focus on governing the Foundation’s metaverse presence. It is a thin series: only the name and number are verified, and it has no anchored metaverse fabric of its own — which is a notable fact, given the name, since the series planned to govern the metaverse has no world of its own. Zero worlds are live on RP1.

The work

The most important thing to understand about this series is the category error it invites. A metaverse DAO sounds like a world, and readers will picture one: a place, with terrain and buildings, that you enter. But the name implies a governance role, not a place. What is planned is a coordinating function: shared standards for worlds across the network, so that a visitor, a builder, or an agent encounters consistent rules about identity, safety, and conduct rather than a different regime in every world. Whether that coordination is needed, and what authority it would have over worlds that belong to other series, are open questions that would need written agreements, not assumptions.

The context for those standards is worth stating honestly, because the gap is the story. As stated on the Foundation site, WorldForge holds 80 worlds — that figure is site copy, not a verified count, and it is labelled as such here. The WorldForge-Browser repository’s worlds file lists 544 worlds, every one of them with no RP1 attachment recorded. Zero worlds are live on RP1: no world in the network has been verified as operating on RP1, and this article does not claim otherwise. The gap between a catalogue of worlds and worlds that are live, governed, and visited is exactly the gap a Metaverse DAO would exist to close — and it is a large gap. A DAO that writes standards for worlds nobody operates would be governing an empty room.

If the DAO ever operates, its agenda would be practical rather than grand. It would need to decide how identity works across worlds, and what an agent must disclose about its operator and its human principal before it acts in-world. It would need conduct rules, an enforcement process, and an appeal for someone who is sanctioned. It would need to decide how builders get space and what they owe in return, what data a world collects about visitors and where that data goes, and how a world is retired — including what happens to its contents and its records. And it would need to decide its own limits: which decisions belong to the DAO, which belong to the series that anchors each world, and which belong to the Foundation as mission gate. Those boundaries, written down and agreed, are the governance product.

The DAO model and stewardship

In the Foundation’s intended model, a series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate routine work inside limits set in advance by humans, and a human Steward Council decides everything that creates a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human’s decision and signature, recorded in minutes. A DAO vote, a poll, or an agent’s readiness flag is not a corporate act.

The Steward Council design in the Foundation’s corporate files is specific: a three-seat council, deciding by two-of-three signed votes, with stewards who are named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials. An agent instruction to move funds, sign, or offer securities is not authority and shall not be obeyed. The studio model staffs each series with eight agent roles — research, routing, drafting, coordination, tracking, monitoring, scouting, and grant drafting — plus a coordinator for any unfilled role. Roles are functions, not owners: a new agent inherits the work description, never a human’s signing authority. A proposal moves through seven stages — intake, mission-fit review, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution with a dated record — and any stage can stop it. No Steward Council for any series is verified as seated, so no series page names one, and until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council and is not operating.

For Metaverse DAO, the stewardship model has a recursive twist: the series that would govern agent activity in worlds is itself an agent-managed series, governed by humans. Agents would operate the DAO’s day-to-day work — drafting standards, tracking adoption, monitoring worlds, coordinating between series — inside written limits. Humans would decide the standards, the enforcement actions, and the boundaries, by signed votes of a named Steward Council. No agent proposal, token vote, or automated instruction would be a corporate act on its own, and no agent would sign, move funds, hire or fire, offer securities, or enact doctrine. That last limit matters here more than usual: a DAO that governs worlds could be tempted to treat a vote inside a world as a decision of the series, and the model forbids exactly that. A vote in a world is a signal; a decision of the series is a signed human vote, minuted, on a written record.

Metaverse connection

Metaverse DAO has no anchored metaverse fabric yet. No coordinates, no fabric URL, no world, and no RP1 status exist for this series, and none is claimed here — which is a notable fact, given the name: the series planned to govern the metaverse has no world of its own. Zero worlds are live on RP1. The six anchored worlds in the Foundation’s network belong to SmartCiti.X, Cognition, Locator, Biota, Empathic, and Diagnostic — none of them is this series, and none of them is governed by this series today. As stated on the Foundation site, WorldForge holds 80 worlds; that figure is site copy, labelled as such, and the repository file lists 544 worlds, all with no RP1 attachment recorded.

That is the complete, verified statement of this series’ metaverse position. There is nothing further to add, because there is nothing further on record: no fabric, no coordinates, no world, no contract, no token, no members, and no votes. If any of that changes, this page should be updated with the dated evidence — a fabric address, a world identifier, a transaction record — rather than with a new claim. Until then, the absence is the fact, and this article reports it. What would be needed first is a defined fabric and a stated purpose for it, and a written agreement with any world it proposed to govern. None of those exists today.

The absence also raises a design question that the series should answer before it builds anything: does a governance DAO need a world of its own at all? A standards body does not need to own a place to set standards for places, and a world owned by the governor would blur the line between governing and competing with the worlds it governs. The honest answer might be that Metaverse DAO never has a fabric of its own, and that its work is done in documents, in agreements, and in the worlds of others. Either answer is defensible — a world of its own, with a stated purpose, or a written record that it needs none. What is not defensible is assuming a world exists because the programme talks about worlds in general, or assuming the DAO governs worlds it has no agreement with.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

  • One line on an internal list: the April 2026 internal W3 series list names this series and its number (S29).
  • The Foundation itself, verified as filed (California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation, Entity No. B20260196664, Articles filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07).
  • No formed series entity, no designation document, no operating agreement, no Steward Council, no bank account, no product, no users, no revenue, no partners, no customers, no sites, and no timeline.

What is planned

  • A DAO-managed series in the intended W3 network, operating day to day through agents within human-set limits, with a human Steward Council deciding, signing, and moving funds, and with the Foundation as mission gate under written agreements.
  • A written statement of purpose, a designated legal form, counsel’s review, a named steward, and a first stage-gate decision — each a human decision with a dated record.
  • A first world with real activity and real stewards, asking to be governed, before network-wide standards are written.
  • Published standards adopted by at least one world, operated for a fixed period, and reviewed in public — adoption, not documents, as the measure.

How it fits the mission

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation, holding the public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence — and acting as mission gate: it sets the mission statement and standards, reviews projects for mission fit, and can withdraw mission approval. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series with isolated assets and liability. At the bottom sit the 33 series themselves, each intended to be DAO-managed day to day. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The Foundation as filed is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, it cannot distribute profits, and it cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. Control, if it ever exists, runs only through written mission agreements between equals, with the Foundation’s board remaining independent. Three mechanisms are meant to interlock across that structure. The Fiduciary Shield is an expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, under California Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630 — not immunity, and not a wall around a company. Open Global Investment is the proposed framework that decouples economics from control, in three share classes for the proposed for-profit track only: Class A with one vote per share and full economic participation, for the global public; Class B with ten votes per share, restricted to aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export controls, and CFIUS review; and Class C with one thousand votes per share, control only, held exclusively by the Foundation. The Windfall Clause is the legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — a percentage of global GDP, with the percentage, the GDP measure, the profit measure, and the scope all still blank for counsel — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination. Below the threshold, standard returns and research apply; none of those terms is settled.

For Metaverse DAO, the mission fit is the Foundation’s own role in miniature. The Foundation sits above the W3 series as a mission gate, not as an owner in the ordinary commercial sense: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head, and the Foundation’s board must remain independent. Metaverse DAO would sit in the same posture toward the worlds — a gate and a standard-setter, not an owner — and it would succeed or fail by the same test: whether the worlds it serves trust its standards enough to adopt them voluntarily, and whether it can refuse a world that fails them. The Fiduciary Shield, the Open Global Investment model, and the Windfall Clause all presuppose that kind of gate working in practice, not just on paper. A Shield that protects benefit-considering decisions only matters if someone is actually making them; a Windfall Clause that obligates all 33 series only matters if the series exist and are governed.

Open questions

  1. Which worlds, if any, have asked to be governed by shared standards — and what would those standards cover first?
  2. Where does Metaverse DAO’s authority end and each anchoring series’ authority begin, in writing — and who prevails when they disagree?
  3. What would count as adoption: how many worlds, with what activity, operating under the standards for how long?
  4. Does Metaverse DAO need a world of its own, or should it govern only through documents and agreements with worlds that belong to others?
  5. How would identity, conduct, enforcement, and appeal work across worlds — and what data would a world collect about visitors, and where would it go?
