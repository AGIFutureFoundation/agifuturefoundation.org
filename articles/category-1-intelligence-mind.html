Intelligence & Mind

Three planned W3 series sit at the centre of the Foundation’s architecture, because they are about the mind itself: how people learn, how people understand one another, and how people find and check what they know. Cognition.X, Empathic.X, and InfoGurus are Series 1, 2, and 33 in the April 2026 internal W3 series list — the first, the second, and the last names on it — and each one is intended and planned, not formed, funded, or offered.

Status — please read first: Every series in this category is named in the April 2026 internal W3 series list. W3 LLC formation and per-series designation are not verified in filings — each series is intended/planned, not formed, funded, or offered.

What this category is about

The category is called Intelligence & Mind because its three series are about capacities, not industries. Cognition.X is about learning and understanding: how a person takes in something new, holds it, reasons with it, and uses it. Empathic.X is about empathy: recognising what another person is feeling and responding appropriately. InfoGurus is about knowledge organisation and retrieval: finding what is known, checking it, and putting it in front of the person who needs it. Together they cover the inward arc of intelligence — learn, understand, know — and they are the reason this category comes first in the article series, even though one of its members sits at the very end of the W3 list.

It is worth noticing that the three series are uneven in what is verified about them, and the unevenness is itself informative. Cognition.X has a published direction — Flow Zone education, as stated on the Foundation site — and an anchored metaverse campus at 45.483, 18.572, with its fabric JSON live on GitHub Pages and its assets not yet hosted. Empathic.X has almost nothing verified beyond its name, but it too has an anchored metaverse fabric, at 47.440, −179.350, in the same partial state. InfoGurus has neither a published direction beyond its name nor an anchored world. The category therefore contains the full range of the W3 programme in miniature: one series with a direction and a partial world, one with a partial world and no direction, and one with a name and nothing else. The articles in this category do not smooth that range over; each one reports exactly what is verified for its series and no more.

What the three series share, beyond the category label, is a set of questions that no other category raises in quite the same way. A learning series must answer whose knowledge it teaches and what data it keeps on a learner. An empathy series must answer where supporting human empathy ends and simulating feeling begins, and what its data policy says about emotional signals. An information series must answer what accuracy and sourcing standard every claim carries, and where general information ends and professional advice begins. Those are governance questions before they are product questions, and they belong to the human stewards who would one day run these series — not to the agents that might operate their tools. That is why each article in this category spends as much space on stewardship and open questions as it does on the work itself.

There is also a structural point worth making about why these three series matter to the rest of the programme. Almost every other W3 series depends on people who can learn, understand one another, and check what they know: diagnostics depends on clinicians who understand what a system tells them, robotics depends on operators who can learn new skills, and every DAO in the network depends on stewards who can read a proposal and judge it. If the intelligence-and-mind series fail — if learning tools mislead, empathy tools manipulate, or information tools cannot be checked — the failure spreads, because the rest of the programme runs on the capacities these series are meant to serve. That is a statement about the domain, not a claim about any series’ status: none of the three is operating, and none has a verified team, budget, or user.

How the category fits the Foundation mission

These three series sit in the third tier of the Foundation’s intended three-tier architecture: below the Foundation itself, a filed California nonprofit public benefit corporation holding the mission standard — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — and below W3 LLC, an intended Wyoming series LLC that would house the series as legally segregated sub-series. Only the apex tier is verified as filed; the middle tier and these series are intended, not formed. The Foundation acts as mission gate for the series below it, and its fundraising, as filed, is donation and grant only.

Three mechanisms from the Foundation’s governance pages would apply to these series if they are ever formed. The Fiduciary Shield is the proposed protection for a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside returns — California benefit-corporation status expanding the business-judgment rule for benefit-considering decisions, under Corporations Code sections 14620–14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630. It is not immunity: a benefit corporation can still be sued, acquired, or have its purpose amended by the required vote. Open Global Investment is the proposed model that decouples economics from control — Class A shares with one vote each for the global public, Class B with ten votes for screened partners and sovereigns, and Class C with one thousand votes, held only by the Foundation — but it belongs to the proposed for-profit benefit-corporation track, not to the filed nonprofit, and counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. The Windfall Clause is a planned legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold — to be set by counsel, expressed in the pitch framework as a percentage of global GDP — would flow to humanity through global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination; the threshold, share, recipient, and enforceability are all blank for counsel today.

For this category, the mission fit is also a test. A learning series that teaches badly, an empathy series that manipulates, or an information series that cannot be checked would fail the Foundation’s public-benefit standard before it started, whatever its legal form. The mission gate exists precisely for that test, and it is the reason each article in this category lists its open questions instead of hiding them: the questions are the gate, written down in advance.
