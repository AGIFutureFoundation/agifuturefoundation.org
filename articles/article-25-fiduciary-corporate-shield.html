Fiduciary Corporate Shield

The Fiduciary Corporate Shield is the Foundation's central legal mechanism: a public benefit corporation's expanded business-judgment protection, designed so a board can prioritise safety over short-term profit and defend that choice in court. It is a mechanism and a framework, not a separate company, and it is not immunity. An earlier pitch version used the word that this article will not use as a claim, and the legal documents corrected it.

TL;DR

The Fiduciary Corporate Shield is a mechanism, not a company, and it is expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, not immunity. Its three working parts are a stated benefit purpose in the Articles, a board duty to consider that purpose alongside shareholder interests under California Corporations Code sections 14620 to 14621, and an annual benefit report under section 14630. The Shield as pitched was designed for a for-profit benefit corporation, while the filed Foundation is a nonprofit, so counsel must choose one track in writing before the mechanism can be relied on. It is intended and planned, not in force, and nothing on this page is formed, funded, or offered.

The work

Every board that runs a company faces the same pressure: shareholders, or the threat of shareholders, demanding short-term profit, and a legal rule, the business judgment rule, that protects directors who act in good faith and with care, but that has historically been read as protecting decisions made for shareholder value. A board that slows a product launch for safety, refuses a profitable but risky deployment, or spends money on alignment work that does not pay off this quarter can find itself sued for doing the responsible thing. The Fiduciary Corporate Shield is the Foundation's answer to that pressure: write a public benefit purpose into the company's governing documents, so that a decision which serves that purpose is a decision within the board's mandate, and the business judgment rule protects it on those terms. The idea is simple to state and hard to live by, because the protection only works if the purpose is real, the reasoning is documented, and the board actually follows its own rules.

The mechanism has three parts, and they only work together: the form, a public benefit corporation whose charter states a public benefit purpose; the record, a board that documents its reasoning, so a decision can be seen to be deliberate and within the stated purpose; and the structure around it, the Open Global Investment model and the Windfall Clause, which give the decision its direction and its destination. None of the three is a company. The Shield is the legal layer of a company that would have to exist first. The form matters because a purpose so vague that any decision fits it protects nothing, and drafting that language is counsel's work. The record matters because the planned Governance Tracker, a live audit log of proposals, fiduciary reviews, and compliance checks, is the public form of the board's reasoning, and no live Tracker feed is claimed here until one is actually published.

The correction at the centre of this article matters enough to say twice, in full. An earlier pitch version of the Foundation's story described the Shield as making the board legally immune to shareholder lawsuits when it prioritised safety over profit. That word was wrong, and the legal documents corrected it. The Shield is expanded business judgment, not immunity, and this article uses the corrected wording throughout. Expanded business judgment means this: a board that acts in good faith, with the care of a prudent person, and within a stated public benefit purpose has a stronger defence against a claim that it breached its duties by favouring safety over short-term returns. The court still reviews whether the board acted in good faith, whether it was informed, whether it had a conflict of interest, and whether the decision was within the purpose the charter actually states. A board that acts in bad faith, is conflicted, fails to inform itself, or invokes safety as a cover for self-dealing gets no protection, and a board that is sued still has to defend the suit. The Shield improves its defence; it does not prevent the suit.

There is a second, quieter correction inside the first. The pitch version also implied that the Shield was already in force, protecting a board that already exists. It is not. The Shield depends on governing documents, articles stating the benefit purpose, bylaws setting out how benefit decisions are made and recorded, and a board that follows them, written by counsel for the specific entity. The filed nonprofit Foundation has articles with purpose language, but it is a nonprofit, and the Shield as pitched was designed for a for-profit benefit corporation. Until counsel chooses one track in writing and drafts the documents, the Shield is a designed mechanism, not a live defence. That distinction is the difference between a plan and a promise, and this article keeps it visible.

One further limit belongs in the same passage. The Shield protects decisions; it does not make them good. A board can follow every procedural step and still make a wrong call about safety, and the Shield will not save the people affected. That is why the architecture pairs the Shield with the Alignment Evaluation Gate, designed to refuse shipment of a model that fails its safety evaluation. Both are described as plans here, neither as a live system. The Shield also does not replace governance, does not create a company, has no valuation, no EIN, and no bank account, and does not apply to the filed nonprofit as pitched. What it does, if counsel ever writes it into governing documents, is move the litigation boundary: the line at which a shareholder can credibly sue over a safety-first decision moves, because the law now asks the board to consider the benefit the decision protects.

The DAO model and stewardship

The planned governance model for Fiduciary Corporate Shield is the model described across the Foundation's governance and operations pages, and it is worth stating in full here because it is the part of the plan that most often gets misread. Day-to-day work would be agent-managed: agents would operate routine work within limits set in advance by humans, in the eight roles of the Venture Studio Operating Model (research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting). Those roles describe functions, not people and not owners, and no agent holds any of them for Fiduciary Corporate Shield today.

Above the agents sits the human gate. A human Steward Council would decide, sign, and move funds: a DAO vote, a token vote, or an agent's readiness flag is not a corporate act, and only a named human acting within signed, written authority can sign a contract, move money, issue securities, or enact a charter. The Council would set the limits inside which agents may act, review what agents did, and remain accountable.

The hard limits bind every agent role equally. Agents NEVER sign, move funds, hire or fire, offer securities, or enact doctrine or charters. Agents prepare; humans execute. For Fiduciary Corporate Shield, that model is planned, not operating: no Steward Council is verified as seated, no operating agreement is in the files reviewed, and no published set of agent limits exists.

  • The correction itself: the legal documents' replacement of the pitch wording with expanded business judgment.
  • The Foundation's filing record: Entity No. B20260196664, filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07.
  • The only repositories are FiduciaryCorporateShield (two-line README, title only) and shield-site (landing stub).

What is planned

  • Counsel's written choice of one track: filed nonprofit, or a new for-profit benefit corporation.
  • Governing documents that state the benefit purpose and the decision record: articles, bylaws, and a benefit report under the relevant statute.
  • A published Governance Tracker and Alignment Evaluation Gate, as live records rather than plans.
  • The Windfall Clause written into governing documents, with its threshold, measure, share, and recipient set; currently all blank for counsel.
  • A seated board that follows the documents and publishes the first benefit report.

No timeline is given for any planned item, because no timeline is verified. Each planned item would need its own document and decision before it could be stated here as fact.

How it fits the mission

The Shield is the legal layer of the apex tier: the governance and mission tier the Foundation occupies in the intended three-tier structure. It is not a cell in the commercial tier, not a W3 series, and not a venture with a product. Its relationship to the series is that it is meant to protect the board that stewards them. Open Global Investment keeps governance and economics apart: Class A with one vote and full economics for the global public, Class B with ten votes for restricted partners and sovereigns, Class C with 1,000 votes, control-only, for the Foundation. The Windfall Clause removes the incentive to fight the benefit purpose, by pre-committing profits above a threshold set as a percentage of global GDP to humanity through the four Benefit Buckets. The three mechanisms interlock, and none is in force until counsel writes it into governing documents.

The intended structure has three tiers. At the top sits the AGI Future Foundation, a filed California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation (Entity No. B20260196664, Articles filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07), as governance and mission steward. In the middle, in the intended plan, sits W3 LLC, a Wyoming series LLC, and below it the 33 named W3 sub-series in the April 2026 internal list. Only the top tier is verified as filed. The middle and bottom tiers are intended, not established, and nothing on this page should be read as saying the Foundation owns, controls, or has formed Fiduciary Corporate Shield.

Three mechanisms hold the intended structure together, and each is a plan with work still to do. The Fiduciary Shield is expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions under California Corporations Code sections 14620 to 14621, with an annual benefit report under section 14630. It is not immunity: a board can still be sued and must still act in good faith. Open Global Investment is the proposed for-profit benefit-corporation framework only: Class A, one vote and full economics, open to the global public; Class B, ten votes, for restricted aligned partners and sovereigns screened for sanctions, export-control, and foreign-investment rules; Class C, 1,000 votes, control-only, reserved to the Foundation. Its suggested first tranche is a SAFE of $500,000 to $1,500,000 with a suggested cap of $8 million to $15 million, illustrative only, and a second priced tranche whose price would be set then, not today. The PBC track and the AGI Corp SAFE are alternative issuers, never both for the same round, and nothing has been issued under either. The Windfall Clause is a pre-commitment, hardwired before profits exist, that profits above a threshold set as a percentage of global GDP would be shared with humanity through four Benefit Buckets, Global Public Goods, Healthcare Access, Energy Infrastructure, and Technological Self-Determination, for the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Its threshold measure, scope, share, and recipient are blank for counsel, and it can be amended only by supermajority plus Class C.

Two cautions belong in the same passage. First, the filed Foundation is a nonprofit: it cannot offer equity, a SAFE, or a profit share, cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head, and its board must remain independent, with control only through written mission agreements designed with counsel. The for-profit track is separate and proposed, and counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. Second, the Foundation's fundraising as filed is donation and grant only. No suggested figure here is an offering, a price, a term, or a promise of any return, and suggested figures are never added into a family total.
