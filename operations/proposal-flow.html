Operations · Proposal Flow

How a proposal moves

From intake to record: a proposal is drafted by agents, screened for mission fit by the Foundation, reviewed by the Steward Council, decided by humans with signed votes, executed only as decided, and filed with a dated receipt. Any step can stop it — and a stopped proposal is a working system, not a broken one.

The flow at a glance

A proposal moves through seven stages: intake, mission-fit review, legal assignment, series designation, Steward Council review, human decision, and execution and record. Agents do the drafting and preparation at every stage; humans do the deciding where a decision is made. The sections after it explain each stage, what evidence it produces, and what happens when a proposal fails at that stage.

Stage 1 — Intake: the proposal is filed

Every new project starts the same way: a proposal is filed with the facts the intake checklist requires — project name, description, founder or lead, intended world or product, and funding source (donation, grant, investment, or revenue), with one primary source named. Agents do the intake work: research synthesis scans and drafts, proposal drafting writes the proposal in its defined shape, and task routing makes sure it reaches the right reviewers. But intake is not approval. A filed proposal is a candidate, and the register row opened for it is a tracking row, not a filing: a series exists only when its master LLC's operating agreement designates it, its books are segregated, and, where the statute requires it, its designation is filed or recorded. The intake checklist is a template, and counsel is to confirm the Wyoming and Delaware requirements for each master before any designation is treated as complete. A proposal without a named funding source, founder, or lead is incomplete at intake and goes back for completion rather than forward to review.

Stage 2 — Mission-fit review: the Foundation decides

The second stage is the Foundation's mission-fit review — the first human decision in the flow. The question is whether the project advances safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI under the Foundation's mission and standards. The answer is recorded as approved, declined, or approved with conditions, and minuted with reasons. A declined project does not get a series number — without exception, even for a commercially promising project: mission fit is a gate, not a preference. The review is done by the Foundation board, not by agents or the studio; agents may prepare the checklist and supporting material, but they do not pass or fail a project on mission grounds. Conditions are written down with the approval, so later stages can check whether they were met; a project whose conditions are never met returns to the Foundation for a fresh decision. This stage is also where the Fellowship boundary is enforced: the Fellowship is a separate church, not a series, and is not put in the series registers — it has its own governance records, and any partnership or support runs under written agreements with an independence clause, not through intake.

Stage 3 — Legal assignment: one master, one series

Once a project passes mission fit, it is assigned to one master LLC — and only one. Existing family series sit under the intended Wyoming W3 LLC; all new projects go into the intended Delaware cohort series company, under the naming convention C1S1, C1S2, C1S3, where C1S1 means Cohort 1, Series 1. The assignment is minuted, with the reason recorded — for new projects, the standing direction that all new projects go to Delaware, unless counsel advises otherwise for a specific project. Two rules guard this stage. First, a project must never be assigned to both masters: Wyoming and Delaware series are different statutes, different master LLCs, different registered agents, and different books, and the two states' series do not automatically recognize each other's liability shields, so counsel must confirm cross-state treatment. Second, the cohort label is not a legal name. "C1S1" is a reference label; the legal name is the master LLC's name plus the series designation in its operating agreement — for example, "[Delaware Master LLC], Series C1S1". Contracts, bank accounts, and SAFEs are never signed, opened, or issued in the name "C1S1" alone, or in a project nickname alone. The Delaware master LLC's name is still to be chosen and its registered agent still to be named; until those exist, no Delaware designation can be made at all.

Stage 4 — Series designation: the company is formed on paper

Designation is where the project stops being a proposal and starts being a series — on paper. The master LLC's operating agreement is amended or appended to designate the series, with its purpose, assets, and liabilities segregated on paper. A DAO charter is written for that series: series name and nickname, purpose, master LLC, agent team with roles and limits, proposal process, Steward Council and its two-of-three rule, funds rules, reporting duties, and exit and wind-down terms agreed in writing before launch rather than after a dispute. The Steward Council is named: three consenting humans, holding signing keys. The legal manager or signer is named — a human, or AGI Corp under a written management agreement — authorized to sign contracts and bank documents for that series. Segregated books and a segregated bank account are opened for the series, only after designation, and no series funds go into the master LLC's general account, AGI Corp's account, or the Foundation's account. Intellectual property is settled on day one: who owns the project's IP — series, AGI Corp, Foundation, or a contributor — by signed assignment or license, not by assertion. Until the Council is named, consenting, and holding keys, the series is "Proposed", not "Operating", and is capped at Gate 1: it cannot pass to Build as an operating company. Until a threshold is set, all spending requires Council approval, and the default agent spending limit is zero dollars without Council approval.

Stage 5 — Steward review: the Council examines the proposal

With the series designated and the Council seated, proposals go to the Steward Council: three humans deciding by two-of-three signed votes on budget, charter, funds, major contracts, and pivot or kill recommendations. The review is the Council's own work: agents prepare the proposal, the evidence, and the options, and the Council judges them. A DAO vote may run alongside as an advisory signal, but it is not the decision — a DAO vote does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities; only the series' legal manager or Steward Council, with signed authority, does that. A token vote, a poll, or an agent proposal is not a corporate act. The review also covers the funding gate: donation and grant funding through the Foundation gate, investment and SAFE funding through the AGI Corp gate, never the other way round, never from an unformed series. The review ends when the Council is ready to decide; the decision is the next stage, not part of the review: review prepares a decision, it does not make one.

Stage 6 — Human decision: approve, amend, decline, pivot, or kill

The decision is made by named humans, in writing, with signatures and a date, and minuted with reasons. For a series proposal, the Steward Council decides by two-of-three signed votes. For portfolio matters — allocating studio resources, or a final kill or pivot where the series agreements give the studio that right — the AGI Corp board decides. For mission matters, the Foundation board decides, and it can withdraw mission approval; that project then loses Foundation support and branding, even if AGI Corp wants to continue it commercially. For partnership or doctrinal matters, the Fellowship board decides for its own programs. The available decisions are always the same: approve, approve with conditions, amend, decline, pivot, or kill. A decision to kill is recorded with reasons, and the model treats killing a project as a studio success, not a failure to hide. The decision also names what happens next: who executes, by when, and what evidence must be filed. A decision that names no executor and no deadline is incomplete, and the record should say so.

Stage 7 — Execution and record: only the decided step, then the receipt

Execution is deliberately narrow: agents carry out the decided step, and only that step, in the form it was decided — operating inside an approved budget, running an approved launch checklist, executing the amended version of an amended decision. Anything needing a signature, a bank transfer, a filing, a hire, a securities offer, or a doctrinal act stops and goes back to a human, because those acts are human-only by definition. An agent that finds the decided step cannot be executed as written does not improvise a substitute; it reports back, and the flow returns to the Council for a fresh decision. Then comes the record. Every proposal leaves a dated receipt: who drafted it, which agent and which human, what was decided, who decided it and when, what was executed, and what remains open. The portfolio dashboard is the standing form of that record — one row per series, with gate, lead human, lead agents, actual funds in and out, actual revenue or "none yet", actual mission outcome, risks and blockers, and the next gate or decision. It is updated monthly, reports actuals only, never lists an unsigned partner, customer, or investor, and never carries the Fellowship as a portfolio row. The loop then repeats.

The funding gates inside the flow

Two funding gates sit inside the proposal flow, and confusing them is the fastest way to break the law in this structure. The donation and grant gate runs through the Foundation: donations and grants may fund a studio project only as a grant serving a charitable purpose, with reporting — not as studio equity, not as a SAFE, not as a subsidy for a commercial series' losses, unless counsel advises a specific lawful structure in writing. No SAFE, equity, profit share, or return may be issued by the filed nonprofit Foundation or by the Fellowship church, and no tax-deductible donation may be claimed, because no IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter is in the files for either. The investment gate runs through AGI Corp: a SAFE or equity investment is possible only where the issuer is a formed for-profit, board-authorized, counsel-reviewed, with cap, discount, and round size set — and a project still "Proposed" cannot take investment. The intake rule is a single line: donation or grant goes to the Foundation gate, investment or SAFE to the AGI Corp gate, never the other way round, never from an unformed series. A proposal that names no primary funding source at intake cannot be routed to either gate.

What does not exist yet — the full list

Because this page describes a process, it is worth listing what the process has not yet produced, in one place, without softening. There is no designated series under this intake process — the Wyoming W3 register and the Delaware cohort register are templates with blank rows, and the April 2026 internal list of 33 named W3 sub-series is an internal printout, not a Wyoming filing and not a designation. There is no Delaware master LLC name, no Delaware registered agent, and no Delaware operating agreement on file. There is no Wyoming W3 operating agreement on file. No Steward Council has been seated for any series, no DAO charter has been signed under this process, and no legal manager has been named for any series. No segregated series bank account has been opened under the studio model, and no money has moved under it. No SAFE, equity, or grant has been issued or made under it. No project has passed any stage gate under it, because no project has entered it. The valuations and raises in the corporate paperwork are planning figures, labelled as suggested, with no revenue, appraisal, or signed terms behind them. None of this means the process is wrong; it means the process is untested, and presenting it as tested would be the error. When the first proposal does move through it, the record at stage seven is what will prove it worked — not this page.
