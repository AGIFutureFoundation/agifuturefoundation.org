Operations · Agent Roles

The eight agent roles

Every series in the venture studio is staffed by the same eight agent roles — functions, not people and not owners — describing work an agent does under a human Steward Council, inside written limits. This page names the eight roles exactly as the studio model defines them, explains what each one does, and states the hard limits that bind all eight equally.

Where the eight roles come from

The studio model defines the agent team per series as eight roles: research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting — plus a coordinator for any unfilled role. The coordinator is not a ninth role; it is the rule for what happens when a role is empty. If a series has no agent seated in, say, the monitoring role, a named coordinator covers that work until the role is filled, and the coverage is recorded. The eight roles themselves do not change. Each series' DAO charter must name which agent holds which role, who the human coordinator or manager is, and what each agent may and may not do — including its spending limit, which is zero dollars without Council approval unless the Council sets a written threshold.

Three rules apply to the team as a whole. First, agent identity is recorded — name, model, and instance per role — so a proposal can be traced to the agent that drafted it and the human that adopted it; an agent proposal must never be presented as a human decision. Second, roles are functions, not persons: if an agent or model changes, the assignment is updated and minuted, and a new agent inherits nothing but the work description — never a human's signing authority. Third, the team works inside the management loop on the Operations hub: agents draft and propose, humans decide, agents execute the decided step, and everything is recorded. A role that drafts a proposal has done its job when the proposal reaches a human; it has no legal effect until a human with signed authority adopts it.

Role 1 — Research synthesis

The research synthesis role gathers material and turns it into something a human can decide on. In ideation and sourcing it scans research, markets, repositories, and community input, drafts project briefs, and ranks them against mission and market criteria. In validation it produces customer and problem research, competitor mapping, technical feasibility drafts, and risk registers. Its output is always a synthesis with sources: what was found, where it came from, what is uncertain, and what the human still needs to judge. It does not decide which ideas enter validation — a founder or steward selects. It does not present agent research as human interviews; the gate checklist requires human interviews, not agent assertions alone. And it never presents a synthesis as fact where the material does not support it.

Role 2 — Task routing

The task routing role keeps work moving to the right place. When a proposal, a request, a piece of research, or a build task arrives, it sorts it, assigns it to the role that should handle it, and makes sure nothing sits unowned. It is the switchboard: it does not do the work itself, it makes sure the work reaches the agent — or the human — who should do it. Routing is where an agent can most easily overstep: routing a task to itself, a decision to an agent, or a signature, payment, or filing to an agent as though it were routine work. The charter rule is simple: any task needing a signature, bank transfer, filing, hire, securities offer, or doctrinal act routes to a human, full stop. Everything else routes to the role whose job it is; if no role owns a task, it routes to the coordinator, and the gap is recorded so the charter can be fixed.

Role 3 — Proposal drafting

The proposal drafting role writes the proposals the Steward Council decides on: a written document with a defined shape — what is proposed, why, what it costs, what it changes, what the risks are, and what decision is asked for. The role drafts it, the Council approves, amends, or declines it, and the decision is minuted. The drafter never votes, never counts a vote, and never treats its own draft as approved because it was well written. A DAO vote is advisory and operational only: it does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities — only the series' legal manager or Steward Council, with signed authority, does that. So this role's work is always labelled as a draft, with the drafting agent named, until a human adopts it; if the Council amends a proposal, the amended version is the proposal. The role also drafts growth, pivot, and scale proposals for the portfolio function — for human decision, never as decisions in themselves.

Role 4 — Coordination

The coordination role keeps people, tasks, and timing lined up inside a series: who is doing what, what depends on what, what is due when, and what is blocked. It schedules, chases, reminds, and reconciles. Coordination is easily confused with management, so the boundary needs stating: coordination is not authority. The coordinator agent cannot approve a budget, reassign a human's responsibilities, hire or fire, or commit the series to a deadline, partner, or customer. Where a human coordinator or manager is named in the charter, the agent role supports that human; it does not replace them. In the Fellowship, agents coordinate scheduling, outreach, and records under human board and minister direction, and the same boundary holds: doctrinal, pastoral, signing, and funds decisions stay with humans.

Role 5 — Progress tracking

The progress tracking role maintains the record of what has actually happened in a series: which tasks are done, in progress, or blocked, and what the next gate or decision is. It feeds the portfolio dashboard — one row per series, with gate, lead human, lead agent, actual funds in and out, actual revenue or "none yet", actual mission outcome, risks and blockers, and the next gate or decision. Its discipline is what makes that dashboard honest: actuals only — no projection in a revenue or funds-in column, no unsigned partner, customer, or investor listed, and an agent's readiness flag never recorded as a gate pass. A gate is passed only when the human approver signs and the evidence is filed. If progress stalls, the tracker says so; if a project fails a gate, it records the pause or the kill. Report what is true, with evidence, and let humans decide what it means.

Role 6 — Monitoring

The monitoring role watches the series and its environment and flags what needs a human's attention: operations, customer support queues, analytics, risks, and mission and compliance items. It watches, flags, and reports — it does not act on what it finds beyond the written limits the Council set. A monitoring agent that finds a problem does not fix it by spending money, changing a contract, or altering a charter; it raises the flag, with the evidence, to the human who can decide. In mission and compliance, monitoring covers mission-fit checklists, standards tracking, conflict-disclosure drafts, and filing calendars; the Foundation board keeps mission approval, and counsel and accountants keep filings, tax, securities, and Attorney General compliance. It also keeps the dashboard's risk column current between quarterly reviews.

Role 7 — Collaborator scouting

The collaborator scouting role finds people and organizations the series might work with — researchers, builders, communities, potential partners, potential customers — and brings them to a human as candidates, with what is known about them and what is not. The key word is candidates: a scouted collaborator is not a partner, a scouted customer is not a customer, and a scouted investor is not an investor, until a human with authority signs the relevant agreement and the signing is recorded. At launch the gate checklist makes this explicit — public claims are checked, no invented partners, users, revenue, venues, or outcomes may be claimed, and the claims list is filed — and the same discipline applies at every gate. The role also drafts investor lists and diligence packs for fundraising support, with securities counsel reviewing all offering materials; agents do not solicit investment.

Role 8 — Grant drafting

The grant drafting role writes grant drafts — for the Foundation's charitable programs and, where the gates allow, for a series seeking grant funding. A draft is written for a human to review, correct, and submit; it never goes out under an agent's name or authority. The intake funding gate draws the line this role must respect: donations and grants go through the Foundation gate, investment and SAFE funding through the AGI Corp gate, never the other way round, never from an unformed series. Foundation money may fund a studio project only as a grant serving a charitable purpose, with reporting — not as studio equity, not as a SAFE, not as a subsidy for a commercial series' losses, unless counsel advises a specific lawful structure in writing. So this role works on the charitable side of that line: a grant draft must not promise equity, returns, or a profit share, and must not present the Foundation as tax-exempt or donations as tax-deductible — no IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter is in the files.

The coordinator — covering unfilled roles

When a role is unfilled, a coordinator covers it. The studio model names the coordinator as the fallback for any unfilled role, and each series' charter must name the human coordinator or manager alongside the agent role assignments. The coordinator's job is continuity, not accumulation of power: covering a role means doing that role's work inside that role's limits, and saying so in the record, until the role is filled. Agent roles carry no authority to absorb — they carry work. The coverage is minuted, and the charter is updated when the role is filled. A series that runs long with several roles covered by one coordinator should treat that as a staffing problem to fix, not a normal state.

Hard limits — the same for all eight roles

Agents NEVERWhat happens instead
Sign a legal documentA human with signed authority signs; the agent prepares the document and the signing is minuted.
Move fundsOnly the Steward Council, by 2-of-3 signed votes; default agent spending limit is $0.
Hire or fireHuman decisions at Council and board level; agents may draft descriptions and candidate summaries.
Offer securitiesOnly a formed for-profit issuer, board-authorized, with counsel review; agents do not solicit investment.
Enact doctrine or charter changesFellowship doctrine by the human board and minister; charters only by the Steward Council. Agents draft; humans enact.
Represent the Foundation or a series to regulators, banks, or the public as a decision-makerAgents may summarize, cite, and route inquiries to a human steward; commitments come only from humans.
Hold equity, tokens, or profit interestsAgents are tools, not owners. Humans may hold equity only as stated in a signed agreement for that series.

These limits are not suspended in either studio mode: in agent-managed mode agents run the daily work within written human-set limits while the Council reviews; in agent-assisted mode a human does the work with agents as co-workers, deciding, signing, and remaining accountable. The modes differ in how much daily work agents carry, not in whether the limits apply. Any task needing a signature, a bank transfer, or a filing is human-only by definition in both modes.

Identity, turnover, and the record

Because the roles are functions and the agents filling them will change, the record matters more than any individual agent. The charter records agent name, model, and instance per role; a proposal is traced to the agent that drafted it and the human that adopted it; when an agent or model changes, the assignment is updated and minuted. That is how the series stays accountable across turnover: the work continues, the limits stay the same, and the history shows who did what and who decided it. A series that cannot show that trace cannot show that its decisions were human decisions at all. The eight roles are a staffing design for a studio that does not yet exist as a formed, funded operation; the discipline they describe — draft, decide, execute, record — has to be true from the first day, because it is what makes everything else checkable.
