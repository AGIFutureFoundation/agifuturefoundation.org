Operations · Studio Model

The venture studio operating model

An agent-managed and agent-assisted venture studio: agents run the day-to-day work of many series at once, humans keep every decision with a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence, and every project moves through six human gates — or it does not move at all.

This page describes an intended operating model from the Venture Studio Operating Model and Stage Gates documents (files 16 and 17), stated by Mike Rice on 2026-11-03. It is a design, not a live studio. Nothing has been formed, funded, or issued under it, no money has moved under it, and no accounts have been opened under it. What the studio is, legally

The studio is a function of AGI Corp, not a separate legal entity — unless counsel advises forming a separate studio entity. Three consequences follow. First, studio contracts are not signed in the name of "the Studio" alone; they are signed as AGI Corp, or as the specific series, as the contract requires, because "the Studio" is not a legal person that can sign or be sued. Second, the studio's operator is the intended Wyoming AGI Corp, whose Wyoming incorporation is itself unverified — an EIN notice alone — so both layers need counsel and filings before either is presented as settled. Third, the studio does not override the Foundation's mission gate: the Foundation reviews every studio project for mission fit, sets mission standards, receives reporting, and can withdraw mission approval even if AGI Corp wants to continue a project commercially. The portfolio is the W3 Wyoming series plus the Delaware cohort series C1S1, C1S2, C1S3 for new projects. A project not in a series is not in the portfolio — it is an idea, not a company. The Fellowship sits outside the portfolio entirely: it may partner with studio projects, but it is never a portfolio company, and studio economics do not apply to it.

The eight studio functions

The studio does eight kinds of work. For each, the model splits the work the same way: agents draft, research, build, coordinate, monitor, and report; humans review, decide, approve, sign, and are accountable. The table below is the model's own split; the paragraphs after it explain the functions where the boundary is most often misunderstood.

2 · ValidationCustomer/problem research, competitor mapping, feasibility drafts, financial model drafts, risk registers.Human interviews, judgment on problem/solution fit, approval to proceed to Build. Financial models are drafts until a human founder or CFO adopts them.
3 · BuildCode, models, worlds, content, prototypes within scope; testing, documentation, demo builds.Human technical lead reviews architecture/security; a human signs IP assignments, licenses, vendor contracts.

Three functions deserve extra attention. In validation, the model requires human interviews, not agent assertions alone — the gate checklist asks how many interviews were done and for the summary to be filed. An agent can draft the guide and summarize the notes, but it cannot stand in for the humans interviewed, and a financial model stays labelled "draft, not adopted" until a human adopts it. In launch, the claims check is absolute: no invented partners, users, revenue, venues, or outcomes, and the claims list is filed before launch approval is signed. Pricing must be set and approved, or the launch is stated expressly as free or demo-only — no invented prices. In fundraising support, agents draft decks, data rooms, investor lists, grant drafts, and diligence packs, and do not solicit investment.

The eight agent roles on the Agent Roles page are the staffing that performs these eight functions inside each series — the functions are the work, the roles are who does it — and the mapping is recorded in each series' DAO charter, which names which agent holds which role, who the human coordinator or manager is, and what each agent may and may not do.

Two modes: agent-managed and agent-assisted

The studio uses two modes deliberately. In agent-managed mode, agents run the day-to-day work of a series within written limits set by humans — drafting, researching, building, coordinating, monitoring, reporting — while a human Steward Council reviews but does not do the daily work. Managed mode is how the model scales: many series, few humans, with the Council's power to stop, amend, or kill as the control. In agent-assisted mode, a human founder, steward, minister, or manager does the work with agents as co-workers: agents draft and research, the human decides, signs, and is accountable. Assisted mode is required where judgment, liability, or religious and doctrinal authority is at stake — fundraising, SAFE issuance, church leadership, steward votes, nonprofit grants. The rule for both modes is identical: an agent never signs, moves funds, hires or fires, offers securities, or enacts doctrine or charter changes on its own. Every such act requires a human with signed authority, recorded in minutes. If a task needs a signature, a bank transfer, or a filing, it is human-only by definition — the agent prepares it, the human executes it.

The six stage gates

A project moves only through human gates. Each gate has a checklist, a named human approver, and evidence that must be filed; a gate is passed only when the human approver signs and the evidence is filed. An agent's readiness flag is not a gate pass. A project that fails a gate is paused or killed, not carried forward on agent momentum.

Gate 0 — Idea (agent-managed, human-selected)

An agent brief is filed — problem, proposed solution, mission alignment draft, market sketch, technical sketch, risks. The Foundation runs a mission screen, recorded as pass, pass with conditions, or decline, and minuted with a date and an approver. A human then selects the idea for validation or not. The evidence filed is the brief plus the mission screen minutes. Nothing is a company yet, nothing is funded, and a declined idea ends here.

Gate 1 — Validate (agent-assisted)

The checklist requires customer and problem research grounded in human interviews, competitor and feasibility drafts, and a financial model draft filed and labelled "draft, not adopted". Then comes series designation: the operating agreement appendix is signed, the register updated, segregated books opened, the DAO charter signed, and the Steward Council named — three consenting humans, with keys held or not recorded honestly. The human decision is to proceed to Build, pivot, or kill. A series with no Steward Council is capped at Gate 1.

Gate 2 — Build (agent-managed, human-reviewed)

Agents build the prototype or demo, test it, and document it, within the series' technical scope. The human checks are the intellectual property chain — who owns what, by signed assignment or license, or "none yet" stated expressly — a security and privacy review by the human technical lead, and the contract rule: all contracts and vendor agreements are signed by a human with authority, none by an agent. The human decision is to proceed to Launch, continue building, or kill.

Gate 3 — Launch (human-approved)

Launch is the most heavily human gate. Public claims are checked — no invented partners, users, revenue, venues, or outcomes — and the claims list is filed. Pricing is set and approved, or it is stated expressly that the launch is free or demo-only; no invented prices. Terms, privacy, and customer contracts are reviewed by counsel where applicable. Launch approval is signed by a named human, with a date. Only then does the project go public. A launch that skips this gate is an unapproved public claim — a governance failure, not momentum.

Gate 4 — Operate / Scale (quarterly Steward Council review)

Once operating, a series is reviewed quarterly by its Steward Council. The quarterly report is filed: funds in and out, KPIs as actuals rather than projections, risks, mission outcomes, and related-party transactions. The budget is approved by the Council on two-of-three signed votes. The decision is scale, continue, pivot, or kill, and it is minuted. Before any new SAFE or equity, the fundraising gate is checked; before any new grant, the grant gate is checked. Gate 4 is a recurring review, not a one-time pass.

Gate 5 — Exit / Wind-down

Exit or wind-down is a decision, not a drift. The board and the Council decide together, minuted with reasons. Intellectual property, data, customer obligations, and funds are resolved in writing before closure. The series books are closed, accounts reconciled, and the register updated to "Closed" with a date and a reason. After closure there is no further fundraising, sales, or public claims, except a factual closure notice approved by a human.

The portfolio dashboard — actuals only

The portfolio dashboard is the studio's standing record, updated monthly, with one row per series: series reference, project, gate, lead human, lead agents, actual funds in and out, actual revenue or "none yet", actual mission outcome, risks and blockers, and the next gate or decision. Four rules govern it. First, never put a projection in a revenue or funds-in column. Second, never list a partner, customer, or investor who has not signed. Third, a series with no Steward Council is capped at Gate 1 — it cannot pass to Build as an operating company. Fourth, the Fellowship is not a portfolio row; it is a partner, and partnerships are listed separately, not as portfolio companies.

Studio economics — structure, not terms

The studio model describes the shape of studio economics and deliberately leaves every number blank, for counsel and the board to set. Studio ownership in a series exists only as stated in that series' operating agreement or management agreement, approved by that series' Steward Council and by AGI Corp's board — the default is that no studio ownership is assumed; each series' ownership is set at intake. Human founders, builders, and stewards may hold equity only as stated in a signed agreement for that series, with vesting, transfer, and tax treatment as counsel and accountants advise. Agents hold no equity, no tokens, and no profit interests; agents are tools and operators, not owners. Management fees exist only under a written management agreement, at a documented fair-market rate, approved by disinterested directors of the series; no fee is charged by default, and none is charged to the Foundation or Fellowship except under the Fellowship partnership agreements. SAFE or investment in a series follows the SAFE gates only: the issuer must be a formed for-profit, board-authorized, counsel-reviewed, with cap, discount, and round size set — and a project still "Proposed" cannot take investment. Revenue sharing, Windfall, and Open Global Investment terms do not exist in the studio model; no series revenue share, windfall share, or broad participation may be promised until counsel drafts those terms in a signed agreement. Foundation donations or grants may fund a studio project only as a grant serving a charitable purpose, with reporting — not as studio equity, not as a SAFE, not as a subsidy for a commercial series' losses, unless counsel advises a specific lawful structure in writing.

What the studio is not

The model also says what the studio is not. It is not a fund: the studio does not pool investor money across series unless counsel structures a fund with its own offering documents, and series funds stay in their series. It is not a DAO legal entity: "DAO-managed" describes operations inside a series, not a legal person that can sign or be sued in place of the series. It is not a church: the Fellowship is a partner, not a portfolio company, and studio economics do not apply to it. And it is not an offer: nothing in the model, a dashboard, or a project brief is an offer to sell securities or a promise of returns. The studio is a way of running many small companies with few humans and many agents, inside human gates, with an honest record.
