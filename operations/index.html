Agentic Management & Operations

How the network is run, day to day

Agents operate. Humans govern. Every page in this section makes those four words concrete: what agents do, what only humans do, how a decision moves from an idea to a signed record, and where the model is still only a plan.

The one rule everything else hangs on

An agent never signs a legal document, moves funds, hires or fires anyone, offers securities, or enacts a doctrine or charter change on its own. Every such act requires a human with signed authority, and the act is recorded in minutes. If a task needs a signature, a bank transfer, or a filing, it is human-only by definition. The agent prepares it; the human executes it. That rule is the boundary that makes an agent-managed venture studio legally possible: a corporation, a nonprofit, and a series LLC can only act through humans with authority. An agent proposal, a DAO vote, a poll, or an agent's readiness flag is not a corporate act. Nothing in this section changes that, and nothing in this section should be read as suggesting otherwise.

The reason the rule matters is scale. The April 2026 internal list names 33 W3 sub-series, and new projects would arrive through Delaware cohort series labelled C1S1, C1S2, C1S3. A handful of humans cannot do the daily work of that many series by hand; agents can, drafting, researching, building, coordinating, monitoring, and reporting across all of them. What agents cannot do is decide. So the work splits deliberately: agents run day-to-day work inside written human-set limits, and humans keep every decision with a legal, financial, or doctrinal consequence. The two modes are agent-managed — agents run the daily work while a human Steward Council reviews — and agent-assisted — a human does the work with agents as co-workers, deciding, signing, and remaining accountable. Managed mode is how the model scales; assisted mode is required where judgment, liability, or doctrinal authority is at stake.

The management loop

Everything the network does day to day runs through one loop, repeated for every proposal, every project, and every series. The loop has four steps, and no step may be skipped. An agent drafts or proposes something. A human decides. An agent executes the decided step — only the step that was decided, in the form it was decided. And the whole thing is recorded, with the agent that drafted it, the human that adopted it, and the date. Then the loop runs again. The diagram below shows the loop; the text after it explains each step and what can go wrong if a step is skipped.

Step 1 — Agents draft and propose

Agents do the preparatory work: they scan research, markets, repositories, and community input; they draft project briefs and rank them against mission and market criteria; they do customer and problem research, competitor mapping, technical feasibility drafts, financial model drafts, and risk registers; they write code, build prototypes and demos, and draft marketing, launch checklists, onboarding flows, and community outreach. Every proposal is traced to the agent that drafted it — agent name, model, and instance are recorded per role — so that a proposal can never be mistaken for a human decision. A financial model is labelled a draft, not adopted, until a human adopts it. A brief is a brief, not an approval. The eight agent roles on the Agent Roles page do this work for many series in parallel.

Step 2 — Humans decide

A named human or a named group of humans decides, in writing, with a signature and a date. For a series, that is the Steward Council: three consenting humans, two-of-three signed votes. For the portfolio, the AGI Corp board; for mission fit, the Foundation board; for partnership or doctrine, the Fellowship board. Every decision is minuted with reasons, and the options are always approve, approve with conditions, amend, decline, pivot, or kill. A declined project gets no series number; a project that fails a gate is paused or killed, not carried forward on agent momentum. Until stewards are named, consenting, and actually holding signing keys, a series has no effective council, and it cannot pass beyond the early gates as an operating company.

Step 3 — Agents execute the decided step

Once a human has decided, agents carry out the decided step — and only that step, in the form it was decided. Agents operate inside an approved budget, run an approved launch checklist, and execute the amended version of an amended decision, not the original draft. Anything in the execution that needs a signature, a bank transfer, a filing, a hire, a securities offer, or a doctrinal act stops and goes back to a human, because those acts are human-only by definition. An agent that finds the decided step cannot be executed as written does not improvise a substitute; it reports back and the loop returns to step two.

Step 4 — Record it

Every exchange leaves a dated receipt: who asked, what was shared, what was decided, who decided it, what was executed, and what remains open. The portfolio dashboard on the Studio Model page is the standing form of that record: one row per series, with gate, lead human, lead agent, actual funds in and out, actual revenue or "none yet", actual mission outcome, risks, and the next decision. Projections are never put in a revenue or funds-in column. A partner, customer, or investor who has not signed is never listed. The record is what makes the loop auditable: without it, there is no way to tell later whether a step was decided or merely drafted.

Who governs what

The humans in step two differ at each layer, and keeping the layers separate is the point. No single human, and no agent, holds all of the powers below.

Three money flows run through this structure, and the paperwork insists they stay separate. The philanthropic flow runs from donor to the Foundation, then to grants or charitable programs, or to a series only under a written grant or agreement that serves a charitable purpose, with reporting back to the Foundation. The commercial flow runs from customer or investor to AGI Corp or to a specific series by contract, into that entity's own account, for its own costs; SAFE money goes only to AGI Corp or to a formed series entity if counsel structures it that way, and never to the Foundation. The management flow — any fee for AGI Corp managing a series, or the Foundation managing a company to mission standards — exists only under a signed management agreement, approved by disinterested directors of the managed entity, at a documented fair-market rate. There is no handshake management. No fee is charged by default.

The structure this loop runs inside

The intended structure, called Structure v2 in the paperwork, puts an intended Wyoming AGI Corp at the top as the head family corporation — family office, holding, and management parent — with the filed California nonprofit Foundation underneath it as mission steward and manager, managing companies to the mission statement and standards. Below that sit the operating series: the intended Wyoming W3 LLC series company for existing series, and an intended Delaware series company for all new cohorts, with new projects entering as series C1S1, C1S2, C1S3, and so on. The design borrows stewardship principles associated with Stanford PACS and effective-philanthropy writing — stewardship, not ownership; mission lock; independent fiduciary governance; segregated funds per project; documented donor intent. It is not a Stanford affiliation: no Stanford entity endorses, partners with, or governs this family. The most important legal tension in the whole design sits right here, and it is flagged for counsel on the Human Gate page: a California nonprofit public benefit corporation cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head corporation the way a subsidiary is. Its board owes fiduciary duties to the charitable mission and the public, not to AGI Corp's shareholders. The Foundation's authority therefore comes from written management and mission agreements, not ownership — and if counsel cannot design that mechanism cleanly, the head must be restructured into agreements between equals, not parent and child.
