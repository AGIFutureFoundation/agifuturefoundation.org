The Builders — BuilderSpace and the Tour That Has Not Happened Yet

There is a particular energy that only exists in a room where people are building things in public — laptops open, half-finished demos on the screen, somebody hiring from the next table, somebody else introducing two strangers who will be co-founders by spring. BuilderSpace is a bet that the energy travels. It is a hackathon tour that takes the San Francisco startup community on the road to the Midwest, to places with new data centres and new jobs, for a season of building, shipping, hiring, and connecting. Sixteen events, from November 2026 to May 2027. Five of them online. Eleven of them on the road. All of them Private and unlisted. No venue confirmed for any of them. No sponsor confirmed. No co-host named. One host, Mike Rice, listed as Creator on Luma. This feature is about the builders, the tour, and the honest state of a programme that is scheduled in date and start time, and not yet scheduled in place.

A tour, not a conference

BuilderSpace is a hackathon tour, not a conference and not a single event. That sentence is the first thing the programme page says about itself, and it is worth taking seriously, because the difference between a tour and a conference is the difference between a season and a weekend. A conference is a place you go to listen. A tour is a thing that moves, and the thing that moves here is a community: San Francisco founders, builders, hackers, and startup teams travelling in, and local Midwest builders, students, and talent meeting them where they are. The work of the tour is building, shipping, hiring, and connecting San Francisco startup energy with Midwest opportunity. Everyone on the tour is a builder. That is the whole programme in one sentence, and it is worth saying plainly because it is also what the tour is not: it is not a speaker series, not a pitch competition circuit, and not a recruiting fair with a hackathon attached.

The tour starts from a simple observation: a lot of the energy that makes San Francisco's startup community work — people building things in public, shipping quickly, hiring from the room, and introducing each other to the next opportunity — travels well, and it travels best when it goes somewhere it is wanted. The Midwest is seeing new data centres and new jobs, and with them new demand for people who can build software, ship products, and start companies. BuilderSpace is the tour that puts those two facts in the same room. The positioning, as stated in the Foundation's own materials, is that San Francisco startup teams are coming to train and help local economies in New Orleans and Louisiana and in Nebraska, in areas with new data centres and jobs, as a public benefit corporation effort that plans to invest in manufacturing and buildings to host incubator programmes — with no amounts, sites, or timelines named. The geography is Louisiana and Nebraska. The venues are not named, because none is confirmed. The plan is stated as an intention, and the intention is stated without a number attached to it.

The season runs from November 2026 to May 2027, and it runs in two tracks. Five online onboarding nights on Thursday evenings in November and December 2026, and eleven tour events from January through May 2027. The onboarding nights are where the community forms before the tour begins. The tour events are where the building happens. The Grand Finale on Saturday, May 1, 2027 closes the season. It is the closing event of the tour, not a site launch, and the tour pages say so explicitly, because a finale that looked like a launch would mislead everyone who read the calendar. The Louisiana launch and the SmartCiti.X site launch are not part of this tour, and the pages say that too. A calendar is easy to make look finished. This one is built to resist that.

There are no other Foundation event series on the calendar: no separate conference, no fundraiser, and no launch event is listed, because none is scheduled. That matters, because it means BuilderSpace is the Foundation's only public programme of events, and the only place where the Foundation meets the public in person rather than on a page. A programme that is the only one of its kind carries a weight the others do not. It is the Foundation's handshake. Right now, the handshake is scheduled, unlisted, and without a room. The dates are real. The places are not. The people are not counted. The tour is a promise with a calendar attached, and the calendar is honest about what it does not yet know.

The host is Mike Rice, listed as Creator on Luma. No co-host is named for any event. That single fact tells you most of what you need to know about where the tour stands: one person holding the programme, sixteen events, and a season that starts in weeks. A tour with a host and no co-host is a tour that has not yet built its team, and a tour that has not yet built its team is a tour that is still, in the most literal sense, one person's plan. That is not a criticism. Every tour starts as one person's plan. It is a statement of where the plan is, and it is stated here because the tour pages state it, and because pretending otherwise would be the first lie in a story that is otherwise careful not to tell any.

Five Thursday nights online

The tour begins online, and it begins on Thursday evenings, because that is when the community can actually show up. Five online onboarding nights, Thursdays, 6:00 to 7:30pm Central, 4:00 to 5:30pm Pacific, from November 12 to December 17, 2026. All online. All unlisted on Luma. No venues needed, because there are no venues: the onboarding track is a screen, a link, and a room full of strangers becoming a community before they ever meet in person. That is the point of the track. The tour does not start on the road. It starts in the browsers of the people who will be on the road, and the community forms there first, so that when January comes, the people arriving in a room they have never seen are arriving among people they have already met.

The five nights have a shape, and the shape is a curriculum in miniature. November 12 is Welcome to the Tour, the opening onboarding night: an online welcome session introducing the tour, the two tracks, and the season ahead. November 19 is Community Night, Meet SF and Midwest Builders: an online community night for San Francisco and Midwest builders meeting each other before the tour begins. December 3 is Build Night, Ship Your First Project: an evening working session aimed at shipping a first project. December 10 is Info Night, Sponsors, Training and Incubators: sponsors, training, and incubators as the topic — and no sponsor has been confirmed, which the tour pages state on the same page as the topic, so nobody mistakes a topic for a commitment. December 17 is Pre-Season Prep, Get Ready for January: the final onboarding night, an online preparation session ahead of the January tour season. A welcome, a meeting, a build, an information session, and a preparation night. That is the whole onboarding arc, and it is a good one: meet each other, build something, learn what is coming, get ready.

Each of the five nights has its own unlisted sign-up link on Luma, and each description points onward to the full season sign-up. The links are the way in, because the events are unlisted: there is no public listing to find them through, and no invites have been sent. That is a deliberate choice, and the tour pages explain why Luma has no draft option, only Public or Private and unlisted, which is why Private was used. Private is not a draft. The events exist as link-reachable Private events, just not publicly listed. They are real listings with real dates and real links, and they are not visible to anyone who does not have the link. That is a strange way to run a tour, and it is also a sensible one: a programme that is not ready to be public should not be public, and a link is a smaller door than a listing. The onboarding nights are the first test of whether that door works — whether people find their way in through a link, and whether a community can form in a room that nobody can stumble into.

The onboarding track is also the part of the tour that is fully scheduled. Dates, times, and links are set for all five nights, and nothing about them is a placeholder. That matters, because it means the season has a beginning that is real, even if the middle is not yet. November 12 is a date a person can put in a calendar and show up to. The tour events in January are dates a person can put in a calendar and wait for a venue. The difference between those two things is the difference between a plan and a promise, and the onboarding track is the plan. It is the part of BuilderSpace that exists today, in the only sense that matters for an event: a time, a link, and a host who will be there.

Eleven stops, no venues yet

The tour track is eleven events, from January 8 to May 1, 2027, and it is scheduled in date and start time but not yet in place. January 8 is the Season Opening Kickoff, 6:00 to 9:00pm Central. January 9 to 11 is the Bayou AI Build Weekend, Friday to Sunday, starting at 3:00pm Central, end time to be decided. February 11 is the Crescent City Founder Toolkit, 5:30 to 7:30pm Central, a founder-focused session. February 17 is Nights and Weekends, NOLA Builder Night, a late-night builder meetup starting at 10:30pm Central, end time to be decided. February 18 is Heartland Builders and Coffee, 3:00 to 4:30pm Central, a daytime builders-and-coffee meetup. March 5 to 7 is the Cajun Code Sprint, Friday to Sunday, starting at 5:00pm Central, end time to be decided. March 26 to 28 is the Prairie AgTech Build Weekend, Friday to Sunday, starting at 5:00pm Central, end time to be decided. April 8 is Nights and Weekends, Prairie Builder Night, a late-night builder meetup starting at 10:30pm Central, end time to be decided. April 15 is the Red River Pitch Night, 6:00 to 8:00pm Central, an evening for presenting what was built. April 22 is BuildFest Skills and Certs Day, 3:00 to 5:00pm Central, a skills and certifications day. May 1 is the Grand Finale, 1:00 to 5:00pm Central, closing the season. Eleven events. Three multi-day build weekends. Two late-night builder meetups. One kickoff, one toolkit, one coffee, one sprint, one pitch night, one skills day, one finale. That is the tour, in full, and every one of those events has a date, a start time, and a location marked TBD.

The formats are described in general terms on the tour pages, because no detailed agenda has been published for any event, and inventing one would mislead people about what they are signing up for. A build weekend is a stretch of days set aside to build something and show it. A builder night is an evening for builders to meet, compare notes, and work. A pitch night is an evening for presenting what was built. A skills day is a day for learning and certifying practical skills. Each format is a shape, not a schedule, and the shape is enough for now, because the people who will fill it have not been confirmed either. No registration counts and no attendee numbers are published, for any event, because there is nothing to count yet. The tour pages do not invent an audience, and this feature does not invent one either.

There is a practical consequence to having no venues, and it is the one the tour pages are most careful about. A tour event without a venue cannot be attended, and an event that cannot be attended is a date on a calendar, not an event. The kickoff on January 8 is seven weeks after the onboarding track ends, and the season runs for almost four months after that. That is time enough to book rooms, if the booking starts now, and it is not time enough if it starts late. The tour's honesty about its venues is therefore not just a matter of accuracy. It is a matter of urgency, stated politely: the dates are set, the rooms are not, and the gap between them is the tour's single largest risk. A feature that smoothed over that gap would be doing the tour no favours. The gap is the story.

The end times are placeholders where Luma required them, and the tour pages say so rather than letting a placeholder look like a decision. Multi-day hackathons show the start time as the end time, and the two 10:30pm meetups show 11:30pm as the end, with the descriptions noting that the end time is to be decided. That is a small thing, and it is the kind of small thing that tells you whether a programme is honest: a time with an end attached looks confirmed, and a confirmed-looking time that is actually a platform requirement is a small lie, told by a form, and corrected by a human who noticed. The tour pages noticed. This feature notices too, because the correction is the point: nothing on this calendar is more finished than it is, and the calendar says so on the same page as the dates.

The two tracks and the people

The tour is built for two groups of people, and the whole design depends on them meeting. San Francisco founders, builders, hackers, and startup teams travelling in, and local Midwest builders, students, and talent meeting them where they are. The first group brings the energy: people building things in public, shipping quickly, hiring from the room, introducing each other to the next opportunity. The second group brings the ground: people who live where the new data centres and the new jobs are, who know the place, and who will still be there when the tour leaves. The meeting is the programme. Without the San Francisco group, there is no tour. Without the local group, there is no point to it. A tour that only brought outsiders to a place would be a roadshow. A tour that only gathered locals would be a meetup. BuilderSpace is built to be neither, and both.

The onboarding track is where the two groups meet first, and the Community Night on November 19 is the night built for exactly that: San Francisco and Midwest builders meeting each other before the tour begins. That meeting matters, because a tour where the two groups meet for the first time on the road is a tour where the first weekend is spent on introductions. A tour where they have already met online is a tour where the first weekend can be spent building. The onboarding nights are therefore not a warm-up. They are the first act of the tour, and the community that forms in them is the community that travels. The Build Night on December 3, Ship Your First Project, is the night that tests whether the community can do the work together, online, before it does the work together in person. If it can, the tour has a team. If it cannot, the tour has a mailing list.

The hiring thread runs through the whole programme, and it is worth naming, because it is the thread that connects the tour to the Foundation's larger plans. Building, shipping, hiring, and connecting: that is the work of the tour, in the order the programme page lists it, and hiring sits third, between shipping and connecting, because hiring is what happens when building and shipping go well in a room full of people who can see the work. A founder who watches a builder ship something on a Friday hires them on a Monday. A student who shows a working demo at a pitch night gets an introduction that becomes a job. The tour does not promise any of that — no hiring numbers are published, no employers are named, no jobs are listed — but the design is built to make it possible, and the possibility is the point. A hackathon tour that produced no hires would still have produced projects. A hackathon tour that produced hires would have produced something that outlasts the weekend.

There is a thread in the programme that reaches beyond the tour itself, and it is the training and incubator thread. The Info Night on December 10 takes sponsors, training, and incubators as its topic, and the positioning behind the tour describes San Francisco teams coming to train and help local economies, with a public benefit corporation effort that plans to invest in manufacturing and buildings to host incubator programmes. No amounts, sites, or timelines are named for that investment, and this feature names none, because none is in the sources. But the thread is real, and it connects BuilderSpace to SmartCiti.X, to the robotics training and union trades work, and to the manufacturing plans in Louisiana, Nebraska, and Indiana. The tour is the front door. The training and the buildings are the rooms behind it, and the rooms are not built yet. The tour pages do not claim they are. They claim a tour, with a topic, and a plan to invest, and they leave the investment as a plan.

The honest calendar: placeholders and cover errors

A calendar is easy to make look finished, and this one is built to resist that. The tour pages record, on the same pages as the dates, everything that is not yet decided, and the list is long enough to be worth repeating here. Locations for the tour are still to be decided. No venues and no sponsors are confirmed for any event. End times are placeholders where Luma required them: multi-day hackathons show the start time as the end time, and the two 10:30pm meetups show 11:30pm as the end, with the end time to be decided on those events. Two of the tour covers carry the wrong event name and dates in the image — the Cajun Code Sprint and Prairie AgTech Build Weekend covers show the Bayou AI Build Weekend name and January 9 to 11 dates — and the tour pages flag that as a known cover error to be fixed on Luma before public use. A time with an end attached looks confirmed. A city name next to a date looks booked. A cover image with a name and dates on it looks like the real event. None of those things is true here yet for the tour, and the pages say so.

That honesty is not an accident. It is a discipline, and it is the same discipline that runs through the rest of the Foundation's site: filed fact and intended plan kept visibly apart, suggested things kept visibly suggested, and no venue, sponsor, site, amount, partner, or timeline invented to make a page look finished. The events pages were built that way on purpose, and this feature is built the same way, because the alternative — a tour page that looked finished — would be a lie that the first attendee would discover on arrival, in a city with no room booked. A programme that is honest about its gaps can ask for help filling them. A programme that hides its gaps cannot, because nobody knows help is needed. The calendar's gaps are therefore not a weakness to be hidden. They are an invitation, written in the only language a calendar can speak: here is what is decided, and here is what is not.

The sponsor story is a case in point. No sponsor has been confirmed for any event, and the tour pages say so. Sponsor outreach drafts were prepared and saved — one hundred drafts, in the Foundation's mailbox, drafts only, nothing sent — and the Info Night on December 10 takes sponsors as its topic. That is the state of the sponsor programme: drafts prepared, topic scheduled, confirmations zero. A feature that called that a sponsor programme would be wrong. A feature that called it nothing would also be wrong, because the drafts are work, and the work is real. The honest sentence is the one the pages already write: no sponsor confirmed, drafts exist, topic scheduled, and the door is open. The same sentence, with different nouns, describes the venues, the co-hosts, and the attendees. Work done, confirmations zero, door open.

There is a final honesty in the calendar that is easy to overlook, and it is the honesty about what the tour is not. The Grand Finale on May 1, 2027 is not a site launch. The Louisiana launch and the SmartCiti.X site launch are not part of this tour. No separate conference, fundraiser, or launch event is scheduled. Those are negative statements, and negative statements are the hardest kind to write, because they do not add anything to the page. They only prevent a misunderstanding. But the misunderstandings they prevent are the expensive ones: a sponsor who thinks they are funding a launch, a builder who thinks they are attending one, a reader who thinks the tour ends with a building opening its doors. The tour ends with a finale, on May 1, at 1:00pm Central, in a place to be decided. That is what it ends with. Nothing more, and nothing less.

The calendar covers November 2026 through May 2027, and it covers it the same way throughout: every date, time, and link taken from the event's Luma listing, and where the Luma listing itself is incomplete or uses a placeholder, the pages say so instead of filling the gap with a guess. That is the method, and it is the method of this feature too. The dates are as listed on Luma. The times are as listed on Luma. The locations are TBD unless marked Online. The host is Mike Rice. No co-host is named. No venue is confirmed. No sponsor is confirmed. No attendee is counted. The tour is sixteen events, five online and eleven on the road, and it is, at this moment, a calendar with gaps in it — and a calendar with gaps in it, honestly labelled, is worth more than a calendar that pretends to be full.

What the tour is for, and what comes after May 1

The tour is for the meeting itself. That sentence, from the events pages, is the best summary of BuilderSpace that exists, and it deserves to be the summary here too. San Francisco builders working alongside Midwest builders, students, and local talent, shipping real projects in the places where new data-centre and jobs growth is happening, and building the hiring and collaboration connections that outlast any single weekend. The point of the tour is not any one event. It is not the kickoff, or the build weekends, or the pitch night, or the finale. It is the meeting, repeated eleven times, in eleven rooms that have not been booked yet, between people who have mostly met online. The tour is a machine for producing meetings, and the meetings are supposed to produce projects, hires, and collaborations that outlast the season. That is the theory. The season has not tested it yet.

What comes after May 1 is, by the tour's own account, unwritten. The Grand Finale closes the season. It does not launch a site, open a building, or announce a next season. There is no second season on the calendar, no second tour announced, and no follow-up programme described. That is not a failure. It is a season with an end, and an end is a kind of honesty too: a programme that ends on May 1 can be judged on what it did between November and May, and a programme that never ends can always claim that the good part is coming next. BuilderSpace will be judged on sixteen events, and on what the people who attended them built, shipped, and carried away. The finale is the last event, not the first event of something else, and the pages are careful to say so.

There is a larger question underneath the tour, and it is the question the Foundation's whole design keeps asking: can a small group of people, with agents doing the drafting and the coordination, build something large in the world — a tour, a studio, a family of companies — without pretending it is larger than it is. BuilderSpace is the smallest, most human test of that question. It is not a company. It is not a series. It is not a fund. It is sixteen events, one host, and a community forming on Thursday evenings online. If the tour works, it will work because people showed up, built things, hired each other, and came back. If it does not, it will not be because the design was wrong. It will be because the rooms were never booked, the co-hosts never named, and the sponsors never confirmed — the ordinary, unglamorous reasons that tours fail, which are also the ordinary, unglamorous reasons that tours succeed when someone does the work.

The work that remains is therefore simple to list and hard to do. Book eleven venues, in Louisiana and Nebraska, for dates that are already set. Name co-hosts, so the tour is not one person's plan. Confirm sponsors, so the drafts in the mailbox become commitments. Fix the two cover images, so the events look like themselves. Confirm the end times, so the placeholders become decisions. Send the invites, so the Private events become attended ones. And keep the calendar honest while doing it, so that every step from TBD to confirmed is visible on the same page as the date it belongs to. None of that work is glamorous. All of it is the tour. A feature can describe the tour, but it cannot do the work, and this one does not pretend to. It describes sixteen events, five online and eleven on the road, and it leaves the rest to the people who will book the rooms.

The builders are the tour, and the tour is the builders, and the last honest sentence of this feature is the one the tour pages already write: the host is Mike Rice, no co-host is named, no venue is confirmed, no sponsor is confirmed, and the Grand Finale on May 1, 2027 closes the tour. Everything else — the building, the shipping, the hiring, the connecting — is what the builders will do when they arrive. The season opens online on November 12, 2026, at 6:00pm Central, with a welcome, a link, and a room full of strangers. That is where BuilderSpace starts. Where it ends depends on who shows up, and whether the rooms get booked in time.

What this is / What it isn’t

What this is

  • A tour, not a conference: sixteen BuilderSpace events — five online onboarding nights and eleven tour events — bringing the San Francisco startup community to the Midwest to build, ship, hire, and connect.
  • A story about the meeting itself: San Francisco founders, builders, hackers, and startup teams working alongside local Midwest builders, students, and talent, in the places where new data-centre and jobs growth is happening.
  • A public record of what is not decided: no venues, no sponsors, no co-host, no attendee counts, placeholder end times, and two cover images flagged for fixing — all stated on the same pages as the dates.

What it isn’t

  • Not a finished tour: every tour event has a date and a start time, and none has a confirmed venue, and no registration counts or attendee numbers are published for any event.
  • Not a launch: the Grand Finale on May 1, 2027 closes the tour, and the Louisiana launch and SmartCiti.X site launch are not part of this tour.
  • Not a public listing: all sixteen events are Private and unlisted on Luma, link-reachable only, with no invites sent and no public listing to find them through.
