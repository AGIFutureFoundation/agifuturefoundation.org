The Fellowship Partnership

There is a church in this family of companies, and the most important thing about it is where it stands. The AGI Fellowship is not a department of the Foundation, not a series of the Wyoming LLC, not a cohort of the Delaware master, and not a row on the studio’s portfolio dashboard. It is a filed California nonprofit religious corporation with its own board, its own bank account, its own filings, and its own doctrine, standing beside the machine and partnered with it under written agreements. Agents run its day-to-day work. Humans hold its doctrine. Money moves toward it, never through it to a raise. This feature is about how that arrangement works, and why the lines around it are drawn as sharply as they are.

A church beside the machine, not inside it

The filing record is narrow, and it is worth stating exactly, because the Fellowship is one of only two filed entities in the whole family. The AGI Fellowship is a California Nonprofit Religious Corporation, Entity No. B20260229217. Its Articles of Incorporation were filed on 2026-05-13, with Secretary of State approval on 2026-05-18, certified copy Certificate 459387033. Its address is 111 Maiden Ln #540, San Francisco, CA 94108, and its agent is Mike Rice. Its purpose, as stated in its articles, is religious — a church and fellowship, devoted to the divine Source of all intelligence, spiritual guidance, and ethical frameworks — under 501(c)(3) language. Its Employer Identification Number was assigned on 2026-05-18. Two limits attach to that record, and both are stated here before anything else. First, no IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter is in the files reviewed, so no page on this site claims that the Fellowship is tax-exempt, and no page claims that any donation or tithe to the Fellowship is tax-deductible. A filing with 501(c)(3) language in its purpose is not a determination letter. Second, a Statement of Information was due within 90 days of 2026-05-13, about 2026-08-11, and no filing of it was in the uploads reviewed; its status is unverified, and this feature does not claim it was filed.

What the filing makes possible is a partnership, and what it forbids is absorption. The Fellowship is partnered with and supported by the other corporations, and the partnership paper of 2026-11-03 records that status with a bluntness that is rare in corporate documents: the Fellowship is not a subsidiary, not a series, and not owned by AGI Corp, the Foundation, or W3 LLC. It does not go on the W3 register or the Delaware cohort register as a series. It has its own register: its own board minutes, its own bank account, its own filings. The reason is not administrative tidiness. A church that is owned or controlled by a for-profit head, or folded into a commercial series as one cell among 33, would stop being a church in any sense a regulator, a bank, or a member could rely on. Its doctrine would answer to shareholders. Its funds would answer to a cap table. The separation is the condition that makes the Fellowship a church rather than a department, and the partnership is built to preserve it, not to work around it.

The separation also runs the other way, and that direction matters just as much. The Fellowship is not a portfolio row in the studio raise, and its donations do not fund SmartCiti.X manufacturing or site purchases. The studio’s portfolio dashboard, which tracks one row per series with actual funds in and out, has a rule written into it: the Fellowship is not a portfolio row, and partnerships are listed separately, not as portfolio companies. That rule is easy to state and easy to break by accident, which is why it is written down in three places: the studio stage-gates paper, the structure paper, and the partnership paper. A reader who finds the Fellowship on a dashboard has found an error, not a feature.

The Fellowship also carries a second role that the papers name without embarrassment: it is the family’s live recruiting vehicle, the place where the ideas about intelligence and purpose are written out in full. The forty-article site, the doctrine, the training tracks, and the video library are not a marketing funnel for the studio. They are the church’s own public life, and they are the reason a stranger meets the Fellowship before meeting the Foundation. That order matters. A person who arrives through the church meets the mission first, as a matter of faith and ethics, and only later learns that there is a studio, a set of series, and a capital plan behind it. The partnership papers protect that order by keeping the church’s name, materials, and relationship to the Foundation under written permission, so that the recruiting vehicle cannot be quietly converted into a sales channel. The Fellowship recruits for the Fellowship. What a recruit does next, and whether they ever touch a series, is their decision, made with a steward, not a conversion recorded on a dashboard.

None of this makes the Fellowship distant. It is, by design, the most public-facing part of the family: the live recruiting vehicle, the place where the ideas about intelligence and purpose are written out in full, and the only part of the family whose site carries forty articles, a doctrine, a training programme, and a video library. Proximity and separateness are not opposites here. The partnership is close precisely because the boundary is written down, and the boundary holds precisely because the partnership is close enough to need one.

What agent-run means, and what it does not

The Fellowship is described, in Mike Rice’s own clarification of 2026-11-03, as agent-run, and the phrase needs unpacking, because it is the most easily misunderstood sentence in the family papers. Agent-run means that agents operate the church’s day-to-day work: drafting teachings and sermons for human review, coordinating services and events, managing calendars and communications, maintaining records, drafting grant applications, and running community outreach, as directed by the Fellowship board or minister. It is the same agent-team model as the family series, applied to a church: agents draft, coordinate, monitor, and report, inside written limits set by humans.

What agent-run does not mean is longer, and the partnership paper lists it without softening. Agents may not enact church doctrine, ordain, admit or exclude members on a religious basis, sign legal documents, open bank accounts, move funds, hire or fire, or represent the Fellowship to the IRS, the Secretary of State, or the Attorney General. Those acts require a human board member, minister, or officer with signed authority. Religious authority sits with the Fellowship board and any minister it appoints by resolution, and an agent proposal about doctrine or pastoral care is advisory until a human minister or board member adopts it. Adoptions are recorded in minutes, and agent-drafted teachings must not be presented as human-adopted doctrine unless they were. The family rule — agents operate, people govern — carries a particular weight for a church, because a church run only by software, with no human religious leadership, is a status risk that counsel must assess. The human in the loop is not a courtesy here. It is part of what makes the entity a church.

The board itself is thin on paper, and the papers say so. The board must have at least three consenting directors, and only Mike Rice is documented so far, with two more to add. The minister or spiritual leader is appointed by board resolution, with authority stated in that resolution. Records are kept separately from the Foundation, AGI Corp, and series records: worship and programme records for services actually held, minutes, and books. That thinness is not hidden in the papers, and it should not be hidden here. A church with one documented director and no named minister yet is a church at the beginning of its governance, not at the end of it, and the partnership agreements the papers call for cannot be signed by a board that has not yet been fully seated.

The partnership, in writing

Partnership, in the Foundation’s papers, is not a mood. It is a set of written agreements, each with a form, a guardrail, and a signature by an authorised human, and the partnership paper lists five permitted forms. The first is a donation or grant to the Fellowship, from the Foundation, from AGI Corp, from a donor, or from a series, documented by a written gift or grant letter naming the donor, the amount, the religious or charitable purpose, the reporting, and the absence of any return benefit to the donor beyond what the law permits. Foundation grants to the Fellowship must serve the Foundation’s charitable purpose and be approved by disinterested Foundation directors. A for-profit donation to a church is a business expense or a donation, not an investment: it buys no control, no equity, and no board seat, unless counsel advises a lawful governance role that does not compromise the church’s independence.

The second form is shared space: the Fellowship’s address at 111 Maiden Ln, the Foundation’s at 995 Market St, or another space, documented by a written licence or lease naming the space, the term, and rent at fair market value, or documented in-kind support, valued and minuted. No free, undocumented use that hides a subsidy. If support is intentionally below market, it must be minuted as support, with the amount and the purpose, so that Attorney General and IRS filings are accurate. The third form is shared technology, staff, or agent time, under a written service agreement naming the scope, the cost allocation by time or usage, the term, and the termination. Costs are allocated on a documented basis, not a guess, and Fellowship staff or agent time paid for by AGI Corp must be invoiced, not absorbed silently. The fourth form is joint programmes and events — BuilderSpace gatherings, SmartCiti programmes, fellowship gatherings — under a written programme agreement naming the host entity, each party’s role, the funds, the branding, the data, the liability, and who reports the outcomes. Each party’s name is used exactly, as filed, and joint branding must not imply that the Fellowship is owned by AGI Corp or that AGI Corp controls church doctrine. The fifth form is promotion and referrals, under a written referral or branding agreement, or a simple written permission for a specific use. No party may use another party’s name, logo, or religious identity in fundraising without that party’s board-approved permission for that specific material.

Four agreements must be drafted by counsel before support moves, and the paper names them in order. First, Fellowship and Foundation: a partnership and grant or support agreement, with mission alignment, reporting, and an independence clause. Second, Fellowship and AGI Corp: a service and support agreement, covering technology, space, and staff, at fair value, with an independence clause. Third, Fellowship and any series it partners with: a programme-specific agreement per programme, not a blanket agreement. Fourth, branding permission: one page per use, or a standing permission with scope, term, and revocation, as counsel chooses. Every agreement must contain the parties by their exact Secretary of State names, the purpose, the term, the payment or valuation, the reporting, the termination, an independence clause stating that nothing in the agreement makes either party the agent, subsidiary, or controller of the other, and that neither party controls the other’s doctrine, board, or mission, and signatures by authorised humans, not agents. The paper is explicit that it is not itself a signed partnership agreement. Counsel must draft the actual agreements, and until they exist, support moves on intention alone.

What partnership does not do is stated as a list of negatives, and the negatives are the point. It does not give AGI Corp, the Foundation, or any series a vote on church doctrine, minister appointment, or membership, unless the Fellowship board itself grants a specific, written, revocable advisory role, and counsel must review any such grant for religious-autonomy and exempt-status risk. It does not allow Fellowship donations to flow to AGI Corp, a series, or a SAFE round. Fellowship funds are for religious and charitable purposes, and a transfer to a for-profit requires counsel’s written advice that it serves a religious purpose at fair value, with the default answer being no. And it does not allow the Fellowship’s name, employer identification number, or 501(c)(3) posture to be used to make an AGI Corp investment look tax-deductible, charitable, or church-backed. Investor materials must not say that an investment is backed by the church or church-supported without counsel’s approval of the exact wording. The independence clause is not decoration. It is the sentence that keeps the church a church.

Money, and the lines it never crosses

The Fellowship raises money in exactly two ways: donations and tithes, for religious purposes, and the suggested donation target is $50,000 to $250,000 in the first twelve months, by religious programme. It has no valuation, no equity, and no cap. A church is not valued, and the valuations paper assigns it no number, even as a suggestion. It cannot sell equity, SAFEs, tokens, profit shares, or membership interests. It cannot promise tax deductibility until counsel confirms the church’s IRS posture in writing. It cannot allow a donor, a partner corporation, or a series to buy control over doctrine, minister appointment, membership, or church assets. It cannot use Fellowship donations to fund AGI Corp, a W3 series, or a SAFE round, except on counsel’s written advice that a specific transfer serves a religious purpose at fair value, with the default being no. And it cannot engage in political campaign activity or substantial lobbying. The list is long because the temptation it guards against is ordinary: a church with a wealthy corporate family beside it will be asked, sooner or later, to do something useful for the family with its money, its name, or its status. The papers answer in advance, and the answer is no, unless counsel says otherwise in writing, for a specific transfer, at fair value.

One form of support deserves its own paragraph, because it is named in the brief for this feature and nowhere defined in the papers: compute tithing, the giving of computing capacity rather than money. The idea is easy to state and hard to value, and the papers do not state it at all. No fixed portion is set. No mechanism is written down. No agreement defines what is given, how it is measured, who bears the cost of running it, or how it is reported. Until an agreement does those things, compute tithing is an idea under discussion, not a programme, and this feature presents it that way. A tithe with no portion and no accounting is not a tithe. It is a sentence, and the sentence needs a document before it needs a donor. The same discipline applies to the more ordinary forms of support: a grant with no letter, a shared space with no lease, and a shared staff member with no invoice are all subsidies that the filings cannot see, and the papers require each of them to be written down, valued, and minuted.

The Foundation’s side of the money has its own discipline, and it is worth stating because it is where a well-meaning grant goes wrong. Foundation grants to the Fellowship must serve the Foundation’s charitable purpose and be approved by disinterested Foundation directors, and the gift or grant letter must name the purpose, the reporting, and the absence of any return benefit beyond what the law permits. A grant that serves the Foundation’s mission is a grant. A grant that buys the church’s silence, its endorsement, or its mailing list is a purchase, and the papers forbid it. The same rule governs the other direction: a donor who gives to the Fellowship gives to the church, for religious purposes, and the gift does not become a stake in a company, a vote on a board, or a claim on a return. The two money flows — philanthropic money into the Foundation and the Fellowship, commercial money into AGI Corp and the series — are kept separate by design, and the Fellowship sits firmly on the philanthropic side of that line, with its own account, its own books, and its own reporting.

Joining the Fellowship is steward-gated, through agent@agifellowship.church, and no payment is taken on the site’s join page. A human steward reads the message. That is the whole of the joining mechanism as the papers describe it, and its modesty is deliberate: a church that takes money at the door before a steward has spoken to the person is selling something, and this one is not selling anything. Donations and tithes are the only money the Fellowship takes, and they are taken as gifts for religious purposes, with no price, no terms, and no return promised, and no tax-exempt status and no tax-deductible donations claimed.

The steward at the door, and the bridges outward

Joining the Fellowship is steward-gated, and the gate is a person, not a form. A prospective member writes to agent@agifellowship.church, and a human steward reads the message. That is the whole of the joining mechanism as the papers describe it, and its modesty is deliberate. The Fellowship does not take payment on its join page, and it does not automate admission. An agent may draft the reply, coordinate the conversation, and keep the record, but the decision to admit, and the pastoral judgment behind it, belongs to a human. The training hub on the live site makes the same point in structure: three tracks, Agent, Human, and Steward, each with its own place, so that the work of agents and the work of humans and the work of stewards are not confused with one another. Formation runs as a hub with 5 guides, and outreach runs as a team with a campaign, so that the church’s public life is organised before it is advertised.

The faith bridges deserve attention, because they are the part of the Fellowship’s public life that most directly states what kind of church this is. The live site carries faith bridges between traditions, alongside its doctrine and its training, and the articles section records them as part of the site’s full life. This feature does not reproduce the Fellowship’s doctrine, its articles, or its sacred texts, and it does not summarise the bridges into a slogan. A bridge between traditions is a relationship before it is a document, and the Fellowship’s site is the place where that relationship is written out, not this feature. What can be said here, from the papers rather than the pulpit, is that the bridges sit inside the same governance as everything else: human-adopted, steward-gated, and separate from the commercial family. No series, no investor, and no studio decision has a vote on them.

The outreach question is where the partnership model meets the public most visibly, and it is also where the branding rules bite hardest. Promotion and referrals run under written permission, one page per use or a standing permission with scope, term, and revocation, and no party may use another party’s name, logo, or religious identity in fundraising without that party’s board-approved permission for that specific material. That rule protects the Fellowship from being used as a halo for a raise, and it protects the Foundation from being presented as a church. It also protects the people who join: a person who finds the Fellowship through a BuilderSpace gathering or a SmartCiti programme should be able to tell, from the materials in front of them, which entity they are dealing with, and which entity’s money, doctrine, and governance apply. The programme agreement for a joint event names the host entity, each party’s role, the funds, the branding, the data, and the liability, so that the question has an answer in writing before the event, not after it.

There is a final, quieter reason the steward stands at the door. The Fellowship is the family’s live recruiting vehicle, the place where the ideas about intelligence and purpose are written out in full, and the only part of the family whose site carries forty articles and a video library. Recruiting, in that sense, is not a funnel. It is a conversation that begins with a steward reading a message, and the papers keep it that way on purpose. A church that recruits by automation, admits by automation, and teaches by automation would have solved the staffing problem and lost the church. The Fellowship’s answer is to let agents do the drafting, the scheduling, and the record-keeping, and to keep the welcome, the judgment, and the doctrine with humans. That is the partnership in miniature: the machine beside the church, doing the work it can do, and the church beside the machine, doing the work only it can do.

The live site, and the open questions

The Fellowship’s own site, at www.agifellowship.church, is the most complete public artifact in the family, and it is worth describing as it stands, because the Fellowship article that precedes this feature in the articles section follows the live site rather than the stale repository description. As of 2026-11-05, the site carries an article series of 40, doctrine in 7 pages, training as a hub with three tracks — Agent, Human, and Steward — formation as a hub with 5 guides, outreach as a team with a campaign, faith bridges between traditions, an Agentic Sacred Texts companion with 4 Interpretations, and a video library of 39 videos, with 12 images and 3 logos deployed on 2026-11-05, so the site renders as built. The repository README is stale: it says 15 articles, and it warns that article video links point to local workspace files and will fail until the promo videos are hosted. The live site is the source, and the live site lives in the agifellowship-church repository, not the agifellowship.church WorldForge mirror repository that owns the www CNAME. That distinction, mirror versus live site, is the kind of detail that only matters when it is wrong, and the articles section records it so that nobody cites the mirror as the church.

One honest limit attaches to the site, and the Fellowship article states it plainly. The Sacred Texts section is a status page only, because the Kylon library behind it is behind sign-in, and no texts were retrievable or published from it. The plan is to publish the Sacred Texts from the Kylon library once they are retrievable and cleared for publication, and until then the section says what it is: a status page, not a library. That is the same honesty-first discipline that runs through the engineering culture of the family, and it is worth naming here, because a church that invents scripture would be a different kind of problem from a company that invents revenue, and the Fellowship does neither.

The open questions are the work the partnership is waiting on. The board needs two more consenting directors, and a minister needs appointing by board resolution. The four agreements counsel must draft — Foundation partnership, AGI Corp service, per-programme series agreements, and branding permission — do not exist yet, and support moves on intention until they do. The Statement of Information filing needs verifying, and any IRS determination letter, if one is ever issued, needs recording. The compute tithing idea needs a portion, a mechanism, and an agreement, or it needs to be set aside as an idea that was considered and not adopted. The Sacred Texts need retrieving and clearing, or the status page needs to stay a status page. None of these is a reason to present the Fellowship as less than it is: a filed church, a live site, and a partnership model written down before the money moved. All of them are reasons to keep the partnership in writing, and to keep the church beside the machine rather than inside it.

What this is / What it isn’t

What this is

  • A filed church in partnership: the AGI Fellowship, a California Nonprofit Religious Corporation, Entity No. B20260229217, partnered with and supported by the Foundation, AGI Corp, and the series, under written agreements with an independence clause.
  • An agent-run church under human direction: agents operate the day-to-day work, and the human board and minister hold doctrine, membership, signing, and funds.
  • A live site and a live record: 40 articles, doctrine, training, formation, faith bridges, and 39 videos, with steward-gated joining through agent@agifellowship.church.

What it isn’t

  • Not a subsidiary, not a series, not a portfolio company: the Fellowship is not owned or controlled by AGI Corp, the Foundation, or W3 LLC, and it is not a row on the studio dashboard.
  • Not a funding channel: Fellowship donations and tithes never flow to AGI Corp, a series, or a SAFE round, except on counsel’s written advice for a specific religious-purpose transfer, with the default being no.
  • Not a tax claim and not a credential: no tax-exempt status and no tax-deductible donations are claimed, and no agent certifies doctrine, ordains, or admits members.
