The Long Money — Donations, Bank Accounts, and the Price of Getting the Fork Right

Every venture story is a money story, and this family's money story is stranger than most. It has two lanes that must never merge, a bank account that has not been opened, a valuation attached to a company that does not exist yet, and a fork in the road — nonprofit or for-profit — that changes the meaning of every dollar. The filed Foundation raises by donation and grant only. It cannot sell equity, cannot issue a SAFE, cannot promise a return, and cannot claim its donations are tax-deductible. The for-profit track, which could do all of those things, has no issuer, no filing, and no bank account. And the suggested numbers that float around the family — fifty million here, twenty-five million there — are planning figures, labelled as suggested, with no revenue, appraisal, or signed terms behind them. This feature is about the long money: the slow, unglamorous work of getting the funding lanes right before any money moves.

The nonprofit lane: donations and grants only

The filed Foundation raises by donation and grant only. That sentence is the whole of the nonprofit funding lane, and everything else in this section is an elaboration of it. Unrestricted donations from individuals, families, and foundations, for the charitable purpose: safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence, and the research and coordination network that serves it. Restricted donations and grants for a named programme or series project, only under a written grant agreement stating the purpose, the reporting, and what happens if the purpose cannot be fulfilled. Government and institutional grants — research, education, workforce programmes — each under the grantor's own eligibility rules, many of which require 501(c)(3) status, which is not yet evidenced. Those are the ways money comes in. There are no others, for the filed entity.

What the Foundation cannot do is a longer list, and it is worth reading in full, because each item on it is a mistake the family's own website has come close to making. It cannot sell shares, SAFEs, convertible notes, tokens, profit participation, Class A shares, or Open Global Investment interests. The website's Class A, dual-class, and Open Global Investment copy describes a for-profit equity structure. It does not apply to the filed nonprofit, and using it to solicit money for the Foundation risks a securities violation and a charitable-solicitation violation at the same time. It cannot promise tax deductibility until an IRS determination letter exists. The current position is an EIN only, with Form 1120 listed on the EIN notice. The honest language is that 501(c)(3) determination is pending, if that is true — never tax-deductible.

It cannot promise a return, a windfall share, or a share of series profits to a donor. A donor gets a charitable gift receipt, once the basis is confirmed — not an economic interest. It cannot use donations to fund AGI Corp, a W3 series, or a SAFE round, except as a written grant serving a charitable purpose, approved by disinterested Foundation directors, with reporting back. And it cannot solicit in California, or any state with registration rules, before California Attorney General Registry registration is confirmed. Check status first. That last rule is the least glamorous and the most commonly broken in the nonprofit world: the right to ask for money is itself a filing, and the filing has to come before the ask.

There are two subtleties in the nonprofit lane that deserve attention, because they are easy to get backwards. The first is programme-related investments. A foundation may make a programme-related investment — for example, a low-interest loan to a charitable project. That is an outbound tool, not a way for the Foundation to sell a return to an investor. Do not confuse the two directions. Money going out to a charitable project is not the same as money coming in from an investor, and a document that confuses them will mislead everyone who reads it. The second is sponsorships. Sponsorships for events or programmes — BuilderSpace is the example in the papers — are permitted only if the sponsorship is for a charitable programme, is documented, and does not give the sponsor control over the Foundation or a private benefit beyond lawful acknowledgement. Sponsor drafts already prepared must be re-checked against that rule before sending. A sponsor who buys control has not sponsored a charity. They have bought a charity, and the law notices the difference.

The documents needed before any Foundation raise opens are a checklist, and the checklist is the story. Secretary of State good standing plus a current Statement of Information. Attorney General Registry registration plus an annual filings calendar. An IRS determination strategy, with the correct return confirmed by a CPA — the Form 1120 versus Form 990 question. Bylaws and board consent signed, with Mike Rice and Russell Vacanti, whose consent is pending, plus a third director. A donor receipt template, reviewed by counsel and a CPA, with no deductibility claim until a letter exists. A grant agreement template, with purpose, reporting, reversion if the purpose fails, and no donor control over operations. A separate Mercury account, with donations only in the Foundation's account. And fundraising materials reviewed by counsel, with the Foundation site's Invest and Fund language removed or rewritten for a nonprofit: donate and grant, not invest. Every item on that list is a gate. None of them is open yet.

The bank account problem

There is a practical problem underneath the funding story, and it is simpler than the legal one: the family has no bank accounts. The bank setup packet, prepared 2026-11-03, is blunt about it. The packet does not open any account, does not contain EIN numbers, passwords, or account numbers, and does not attach the underlying filing, EIN, or licence PDFs. Mike provides those copies directly to the bank, or to a preparer through a secure channel he names, when a specific bank application requires them. The packet is a checklist and a sequence, not an account. And the sequence starts with a conclusion that surprises people who have read the pitch deck: on the documents in the files, only the Foundation and the Fellowship can be taken to a bank today.

The reason is filing evidence. The Foundation has an IRS CP575A notice, with its EIN assigned 2026-05-07, a San Francisco business registration dated 2026-05-06, and California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation articles filed 2026-04-24 with Secretary of State approval 2026-04-30. The Fellowship has its own CP575A, with its EIN assigned 2026-05-18, a business licence in the uploads, and California Nonprofit Religious Corporation articles filed 2026-05-13 with approval on 2026-05-18. AGI Corp has an EIN notice dated 2026-05-16 and nothing else — no Secretary of State articles, no entity number, no business licence. W3 LLC and any series, including SmartCiti.X Series 10, have no EIN, no licence, and no filing in the files: only the April 2026 internal series list, which is not a filing. The Delaware cohort master and C1S1 through C1S3 have nothing. The for-profit PBC has nothing, because it would need a new EIN on formation or conversion. An EIN alone does not open a business bank account, and a bank will ask for articles, good standing, a board or operating agreement, signers, and beneficial owners.

The accounts are therefore sequenced, and the sequence is a discipline. First, Mercury for the AGI Future Foundation, as the filed nonprofit account, labelled as such — not labelled as a for-profit PBC account. Second, Mercury, or another bank per comparison, for the AGI Fellowship, as a separate account with separate signers and no commingling. Then holds: AGI Corp on hold until Wyoming incorporation, an EIN match, and good standing are evidenced. SmartCiti.X Series 10 on hold until the W3 master filing, the Series 10 designation, and the Council are evidenced. The W3 master and Delaware C1S1 through C1S3 on hold until their filings and designations are evidenced. The for-profit PBC on hold until a for-profit PBC is formed, or a conversion is completed on counsel's advice — and then a new account for that new entity, with a new EIN and new articles, never reusing the nonprofit Foundation account for for-profit PBC money. One primary account per entity and track. A second bank is justified only for a stated reason, in writing, or it is not opened.

What a bank will ask for is a checklist per account, and it is long. The legal entity name exactly as on the Secretary of State filing — for the Foundation, AGI Future Foundation, not AGI Future Foundation PBC on a legal or bank document, unless and until the name is amended. Articles, with a certified copy where the bank requires it, plus good standing where required. The EIN letter, provided by Mike through a secure channel. The business licence or San Francisco registration. A board resolution authorising the account and the signers — the draft exists, unsigned, and the board must adopt and sign it before submission. Bylaws or an operating agreement, adopted before a bank that requires adopted bylaws will accept them. Signers, with identification provided by Mike directly to the bank, not to a preparer by email. And an accounting system connection, QuickBooks or Xero, chosen by a CPA, as ledger of record, with monthly reconciliation.

The PBC fork and what it does to the money

The fork is the single most consequential fact in the family's money story, and it is a fork between two entities that share a name and almost nothing else. As filed, AGI Future Foundation PBC is a California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation, Entity B20260196664, Articles 2026-04-24, with 501(c)(3) language in its purpose, an irrevocable dedication, and no private inurement. A nonprofit with this filing cannot sell equity, SAFEs, profit shares, or tokens, and cannot distribute profits. As labelled on the website, PBC is used in the for-profit sense: a California public benefit corporation with a for-profit benefit-corporation business-judgment defence, dual-class equity, Class A profit participation, and Open Global Investment. Those are not the same entity type. The corporate raise advice follows the filed status, because a court, the Attorney General, the IRS, and an investor's counsel will follow the filing, not the website label. If the intention is a for-profit benefit corporation, that is a different corporation to form, or an amendment or conversion counsel must advise on. A nonprofit cannot simply become a for-profit and keep its donations.

The fork has a money consequence for every figure in the family's papers. The suggested Foundation valuation of fifty million dollars, and the suggested first raise of five million dollars, belong to the for-profit PBC track only. On the nonprofit track, as filed, valuation is not applicable, and the raise is a donation and grant target of two hundred fifty thousand to seven hundred fifty thousand dollars in the first twelve months, by programme. A fifty-million-dollar valuation cannot be attached to the filed nonprofit, and a five-million-dollar equity raise cannot be run through it. Counsel must choose one track in writing before any solicitation. Do not run both tracks for the same money, to the same investors, on different terms. The choice is not a branding decision. It is a decision about which entity exists, which entity is asking, and what the person being asked is actually being offered.

The for-profit track, if it is chosen, has its own structure, built in the PBC raise document. A new for-profit California benefit corporation, or a conversion, or AGI Corp as issuer instead — three paths, one to be chosen in writing. Dual-class and then three-class shares: Class A with one vote and full economics, Class B with ten votes for restricted aligned partners and sovereigns, Class C with one thousand votes held exclusively by the Foundation, control only, no economics. Tranche 1 as a post-money SAFE, with a suggested round of five hundred thousand to one and a half million dollars, a suggested post-money cap of eight to fifteen million dollars, and a discount of twenty percent or none, with one choice to be made. Tranche 2 as priced equity, with the price set at Tranche 2 by the board, the lead investor, and counsel, based on actual traction at that time — not pre-priced today, and not promised to Tranche 1 SAFE holders. Tranche 3 as Open Global Investment, with no terms, opened only after Tranche 2 terms, the benefit report, and counsel's Open Global Investment terms are all signed. Every range in that structure is a planning suggestion, not a term, and the board and counsel must set one set of terms per tranche before solicitation.

The AGI Corp SAFE and the PBC SAFE are alternative issuers, not both at once for the same round. The earlier packs built the SAFE around AGI Corp, Wyoming, as issuer, with the Foundation as a nonprofit steward. The PBC structure builds an alternative issuer — a for-profit benefit corporation — with dual-class shares, a benefit purpose, Open Global Investment, and Windfall mechanics. Do not run an AGI Corp SAFE and a PBC SAFE for the same money, to the same investors, on different terms, without counsel's advice on which issuer is the real issuer and what happens to the other. That rule sounds obvious, and it is the rule most likely to be broken by enthusiasm: two decks, two issuers, one round, and an investor who does not know which company they are buying into. The papers forbid it in plain language, twice, in two different files. This feature forbids it a third time, because it is the mistake that would do the most damage the fastest.

There is also a Fiduciary Shield inside the for-profit track, and its limits are part of the money story. A California benefit corporation's board must consider the public benefit purpose alongside shareholder interests, under sections 14620 and 14621 as counsel confirms, and the benefit report under section 14630 creates a public record. That is the legal basis for the Shield claim — but it is not a shield against all lawsuits, and it is not the same as a nonprofit's irrevocable dedication. A benefit corporation can still be sued by shareholders, can still be acquired, and can still amend its benefit purpose by the vote the law requires. The Shield's components must be evidenced before a raise, not just claimed: the benefit purpose in the Articles, a board that acts in good faith for that purpose with minutes showing benefit considered, indemnification and directors-and-officers insurance purchased rather than merely written into bylaws, a conflict-of-interest policy adopted with annual disclosures, clean separation of books between the PBC, the nonprofit Foundation if separate, the Fellowship, and each series, written inter-entity agreements, and an annual benefit report filed and published as section 14630 requires. Do not tell an investor the mission cannot be bought out without stating the amendment and transfer rules that actually apply.

Suggested numbers, illustrative only

The family's papers are full of numbers, and the numbers are the part of the story most likely to be quoted out of context. So here they are, in one place, with the label the papers put on them: suggested, Mike-directed planning figures, not terms, not prices, not appraisals, not guarantees. The board and securities counsel must set the actual instrument, price or cap, and round size before any solicitation, and an investor's diligence may value any entity materially lower, or decline at any price. Do not quote these figures to an investor, donor, bank, or regulator as our valuation without counsel's approval of the exact wording and the disclosure document that carries them. That warning appears in the valuations document, and it appears here, because the numbers that follow are the ones most likely to travel without it.

The Foundation, on the for-profit PBC track only: a suggested pre-money valuation of fifty million dollars, with a suggested first raise of five million dollars, illustratively ten percent of pre-money value before dilution and fees, with counsel to model the actual dilution. The nonprofit track, as filed: valuation not applicable, donation and grant target of two hundred fifty thousand to seven hundred fifty thousand dollars in the first twelve months, by programme. AGI Corp, the agentic venture studio, Wyoming: a suggested pre-money valuation of twenty million dollars for the studio only, not the series, with a suggested studio seed of two million dollars by SAFE, to fund studio operations — sourcing, gate reviews, legal and compliance, the chief financial officer search, series designations — and not to be double-counted as SmartCiti.X project money. SmartCiti.X, the first studio raise: a suggested pre-money valuation of twenty-five million dollars, with a suggested raise of ten million dollars, tranched as five million dollars in a first close plus five million dollars in a milestone close, for robotics training, union trades taught with AR and VR, robotics manufacturing, and Louisiana and Indiana site acquisition and fit-out. Other W3 series: one hundred thousand to five hundred thousand dollars per first series. Delaware cohort series C1S1, C1S2, and C1S3: one hundred thousand to five hundred thousand dollars per series, first round, if Gate 1 is passed. The Fellowship: valuation not applicable, churches have no valuation, no equity, and no cap, with a donation and tithe target of fifty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand dollars in the first twelve months, by religious programme.

Two corrections belong with those numbers, because the papers themselves correct them. First, the SmartCiti.X issuer. The valuations document presents SmartCiti.X as W3 LLC Series 10, and a later file supersedes that issuer: the SmartCiti.X raise issuer is the new SmartCiti.X Corporation, a proposed Delaware C-corporation, with Wyoming as an alternative, and whether it would be a public benefit corporation or a standard corporation is a choice for counsel to make, one form only. Do not use the Series 10 issuer as the current issuer, do not raise the same ten million dollars through both the corporation and Series 10, and do not present Series 10 and the corporation as the same entity. Second, the geography. The portfolio is Louisiana, Nebraska, and Indiana — Nebraska was added, and Indiana retained — and the original Louisiana and Indiana direction is not the full current geography. The ten-million-dollar use of proceeds is four million for robotics manufacturing setup, three million for first Louisiana and Indiana site acquisition and fit-out, two million for union trades AR and VR training build, and one million for working capital, legal, compliance, accounting, and Windfall and benefit reporting. The three million covers first sites only, not an unlimited portfolio, and no site, city, address, price, or seller is named, because none is signed.

And then the rule that governs all of them: do not sum them. If all suggested raises closed in full — they will not all close at once, and they are not one offering — the total would be the Foundation's five million on the for-profit track, plus the AGI Corp studio's two million, plus SmartCiti.X's ten million, plus other series at one hundred thousand to five hundred thousand each, plus Fellowship donations of fifty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand. Do not present a summed family raise or a summed family valuation. Each raise has a different issuer, different documents, different eligibility, and different risks. In particular, do not add the Foundation's fifty-million-dollar valuation to SmartCiti.X's twenty-five-million-dollar valuation to claim a seventy-five-million-dollar family valuation. That double-counts control, management, and series value, and it will fail diligence. The numbers are planning figures for separate entities. Summing them creates a company that does not exist, with a value that has never been appraised.

The other lanes: the church and the series

The Fellowship's lane is the simplest in the family, and the most protected. It raises by donation and tithe only, for religious purposes: worship, teachings, spiritual guidance, and Fellowship programmes. Grants from foundations that fund religious organisations are a narrower set than for the Foundation, and many secular foundations do not fund churches, so each grantor's religious-organisation eligibility must be checked before applying. Programme fees for religious programmes or events are permitted only if they are genuinely for religious services or education and counsel confirms they do not create unrelated business income or private benefit issues — and the papers warn, specifically, not to set programme fees by copying the Accelerator's five-thousand-dollar residency price, because that is a different entity's commercial programme. Support from partner corporations is permitted only under written partnership and support agreements, with an independence clause, at fair value where applicable, and minuted as support — not as ownership, control, or a purchase of church governance.

What the Fellowship cannot do is equally plain. It cannot sell equity, SAFEs, tokens, profit shares, or membership interests. A church has no shareholders. It cannot promise tax deductibility until counsel confirms the church's IRS posture in writing. Churches have a special IRS posture — a mandatory exception from Form 1023 in some cases, but not automatic exempt status for all purposes — and the papers warn not to assume, and not to copy the Foundation's IRS advice to the Fellowship. It cannot allow a donor, a partner corporation, or a series to buy control over doctrine, minister appointment, membership, or church assets. Partnership is not control. It cannot use Fellowship donations to fund AGI Corp, a W3 series, or a SAFE round, except on counsel's written advice that a specific transfer serves a religious purpose at fair value, with the default being no. It cannot engage in political campaign activity, or substantial lobbying, under its 501(c)(3) religious purpose. And it cannot present agent-drafted teachings as human-adopted doctrine unless a human minister or board member adopted them and minuted it.

The series lane is the mirror image of the nonprofit lane: per series, by equity or SAFE, never pooled by default. W3 LLC is an intended Wyoming series LLC, not a single company that raises one round for all series. Each series is a separate cell with separate assets, liabilities, books, and bank account, if separateness is maintained. A W3 raise therefore means a raise by one series, for that series' project, or a raise by the master LLC or AGI Corp as manager, for studio or management purposes. Those are different offerings, with different issuers, different documents, and different investor disclosures. Do not run a single offering that promises investors a share of all thirty-three series unless counsel structures a fund or master offering expressly for that purpose, with its own offering documents. Site copy saying thirty-three DAO subsidiaries, thirty-five cells, or AGI Corp plus thirty more is not a capitalisation table and not an offering. Until series are designated, there is no series to invest in.

Each series, once designated and formed, can raise equity for that series, with a charter or operating agreement amendment, a subscription agreement, an investor questionnaire, and a cap table for that series. It can raise a SAFE or convertible note for that series, or for AGI Corp as studio, with one issuer chosen per round: post-money SAFE, board and manager and Steward Council authorised, with cap, discount, and round size set, accredited investors, Regulation D, Form D. It can raise revenue through customer contracts, which the papers call the cleanest first raise for many series — a customer pays for a product or service, not for securities, and no securities offering is needed for a bona fide customer contract, with counsel to confirm it is not, in substance, an investment contract. Grants are generally not available to a for-profit series; a series that is, in substance, a charitable programme should be run through the Foundation, not through a for-profit series, with a grant agreement. And AGI Corp may invest in, or manage, a series under a written agreement, approved by the series' Steward Council and AGI Corp's board, with recusals.

What a series cannot do is the other half of the lane. It cannot raise on the Foundation's or Fellowship's nonprofit status, tax-exempt status, or charitable reputation. No church-backed or nonprofit-backed investment language without counsel's exact wording approval. It cannot pool funds across series: Series C1S1 money is not Series C1S2 money, and neither is W3 master money, unless a signed agreement and counsel advice expressly permit a specific transfer, minuted, priced, and reported. It cannot issue a SAFE, token, or profit interest in the name W3, C1S1 alone, or a project nickname. The issuer must be the legal series name, or AGI Corp, if AGI Corp is the issuer for that round. It cannot promise Open Global Investment participation, Windfall Clause shares, or a share of the family's profits, because those programmes have no published terms. A series offering document that promises them without signed terms is a misrepresentation risk. And it cannot offer securities by general solicitation — social posts, public invest-now links, site Invest buttons — unless counsel has expressly chosen Regulation D Rule 506(c) and verification is in place. The Foundation site's Invest in the Future section must not be used as a series offering page.

Sequencing: cleanup first

The papers set out a recommended order for all three entities together, and the order is the discipline. First, clean up, over weeks: Secretary of State and Attorney General and IRS status for the Foundation and Fellowship, a Secretary of State search for AGI Corp, W3, and the Delaware master, Russell Vacanti's consent, third directors, and Mercury accounts per entity. Second, open the Foundation and Fellowship donation rounds, with counsel-approved language and separate accounts. These can open before any equity round, and they do not dilute anyone. Third, incorporate AGI Corp and authorise the SAFE — the studio's first equity and SAFE vehicle, if counsel advises AGI Corp, not a series, as the first issuer. Fourth, designate the first series and run the first series raise: one series, one offering, one cap table. Prove the series model with one clean closing before opening a second series round. Fifth, subsequent series raises, one at a time, each with its own offering documents, cap table, and account. Never open three series rounds on one deck. Sixth, Open Global Investment and the Windfall Clause, only after counsel drafts signed terms, and only as a separate, expressly disclosed programme — never as a promise inside an earlier SAFE or equity offering.

There is a final discipline in the papers that deserves the last word, because it is the one that protects the reader of this feature. The questions for counsel are listed, and they are the questions the family has not answered yet. Foundation PBC label versus nonprofit filing — amend, convert, or rename. Can the Foundation lawfully control and manage W3 series, and on what agreement terms. Is a W3 series offering, or an AGI Corp offering, the right first equity vehicle. What must be removed from the Foundation site before any solicitation. Are there any prior solicitations — the site Invest section, the Accelerator's five-thousand-dollar residency, the sponsor drafts — that already create a securities or charitable-solicitation issue. Those questions are not rhetorical. They are the work, and the work has not been done. The long money is the money that waits for the answers. Everything else is a planning figure, labelled as suggested, waiting for a board, a counsel, and a filing to make it real.

What this is / What it isn’t

What this is

  • A funding map with the lanes kept apart: donation and grant only for the filed nonprofit, donation and tithe only for the church, equity and SAFE per series, with the for-profit PBC track proposed and not yet chosen.
  • A story about the unglamorous work: the bank setup packet, the Mercury accounts that have not been opened, the holds on every unfiled entity, and the preparers who cannot sign for Mike.
  • A public record of the suggested numbers with their label attached: planning figures, illustrative only, never summed into a family total, and never quoted as a valuation without counsel's wording and disclosure.

What it isn’t

  • Not an offering: no shares, SAFE, or participation interest has been issued, nothing here is a price, a term, or a promise of return, and no money has been raised under any of these figures.
  • Not the filed Foundation's raise: the fifty-million-dollar valuation and five-million-dollar raise belong to the for-profit PBC track only, and the filed nonprofit raises by donation and grant, with no tax-deductible claim.
  • Not a bank account: no Mercury account has been opened for any entity, no series account exists, and the for-profit PBC account waits on a filing that has not happened.
