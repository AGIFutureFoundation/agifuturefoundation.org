What this series is

InfoGurus is W3 Series 33, the last series on the April 2026 internal W3 series list. The list names “W3 LLC-InfoGurus — Series 33” and gives no further description, so the scope on this page is a planned focus drawn from what the name implies, labelled as planned throughout. The name implies an information lane: expertise, guidance, and answers — people, or agents, that know things and help others find and use what they know. That is a broad implication, and the honest thing to say is that its boundaries have not been set.

The breadth of the name is the first problem this series would have to solve, and this page states it rather than smoothing it over. “Information” could mean a reference or knowledge service, an expert network, a research and synthesis function for the rest of the W3 network, or a public information service. Those are different products with different users, different quality standards, and different risks, and a series that tried to be all of them would be none of them well. The first real decision for InfoGurus — before any build — would be to write down, in one page, which of those it is and which it is not, and to have a human Steward Council approve that scope. Until that page exists, InfoGurus is a name awaiting a definition, and this page will keep saying so.

What can be said with confidence is what the series would have to get right in any of those lanes, because information work carries its own specific risks. Accuracy would be the first standard: an information service that states things it has not verified misleads exactly the people who trusted it most, and a service that presents opinion as fact, or a single source as consensus, fails the Foundation's standard before it starts. Provenance would be the second: every substantive claim should carry its source and its date, so a reader can check it rather than take the service's word. And the boundary around advice would be the third: general information is one thing, and professional advice — legal, medical, financial — is another, requiring licensed humans, disclaimers that are actually read, and escalation to a qualified person rather than an answer from a model. None of these are policies in force; they are the standards the series would be expected to meet, listed here so a future reviewer can check whether they were met.

There is also a plausible role for InfoGurus inside the network itself, and it is worth naming as a possibility rather than a plan: a shared research and synthesis function that helps the other thirty-two series find, check, and use information — a service to the network rather than a product for the public. That role would make InfoGurus the most inward-facing series in the group, and it would be judged by a different standard: not by users or volume, but by whether the other series trust its work enough to rely on it. Whether that is the right role, or whether InfoGurus should face outward to the public, is an open question that would need a human decision, not an assumption. No service to the network, and no public product, exists today.

Validation for InfoGurus would be measured in trust and in correction, not in volume. An information service earns trust by being right, by citing its sources, and — most importantly — by correcting itself visibly when it is wrong. The planned sequence is therefore: written scope first, choosing one lane and naming the lanes it excludes; then a small, testable service, with sources and dates on every claim and a published corrections log; then review by the people it serves, including whether they found it accurate and useful; and only then any discussion of scale. A service that grows before it has published its first correction has not yet learned the habit that makes information work trustworthy. Until that habit is demonstrated, InfoGurus remains a planned focus, and this page keeps it described that way.

Governance: DAO-managed day to day, humans decide

Under the Foundation's intended venture-studio model, each W3 series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate the series within limits set by humans, and a human Steward Council decides. That division is deliberate and non-negotiable in the model. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human's decision and signature.

The Foundation sits above the W3 series as a mission gate, not as an owner in the ordinary commercial sense: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head, and the Foundation's board must remain independent. Control in the intended model runs through written mission agreements and standards the series agree to, with counsel to design the exact structure. The Foundation's public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — is the standard every series is meant to be measured against, and the Windfall Clause framework is planned to apply across the W3 series: a legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold (to be set by counsel, expressed in the pitch framework as a percentage of global GDP) would flow to humanity through global public goods. The threshold, measurement, recipient, and enforceability are all blank for counsel today, and no series page states them as settled terms.

Governance of a series, once designated, would also need a written operating agreement, a named Steward Council, a compliance calendar, and separate accounts and records per series. None of that exists for this series yet, because the series itself does not exist yet as a legal entity. The governance described on this page is the intended model, not a description of a operating body. Anyone reading this page who wants to help shape that governance should contact the Foundation (see below) rather than assuming any of it is already in force.

Open questions this series must answer before it can proceed

  1. Which information lane is in scope — reference, expert network, network research service, or public service — and which lanes are excluded?
  2. What accuracy and sourcing standard would every claim carry, and how would corrections be published?
  3. Where does general information end and professional advice begin, and who, by name, is accountable for that boundary?

These are not rhetorical questions. Each one needs a written answer, approved by a named human, before the series passes its first stage gate. If you can help answer one of them, the contact below is the route.
