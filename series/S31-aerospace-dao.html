What this series is

Aerospace DAO is W3 Series 31. The April 2026 internal W3 series list names “W3 LLC-Aerospace DAO — Series 31” and gives no further description, so the scope on this page is a planned focus drawn from what the name implies, labelled as planned throughout. The name implies an aerospace lane: AI for flight, for aircraft and spacecraft systems, and for the ground systems that support them. That is a domain where the safety bar is the highest of any in the network, and the planned focus has to start from that fact rather than from enthusiasm about flight.

Aerospace is unlike the other physical-AI lanes in one decisive respect: it is already one of the most regulated human activities on earth, and any AI that touches flight would enter that regulatory world rather than displacing it. Airworthiness certification, air-traffic control, export controls, and sanctions screening are not obstacles to be routed around; they are the conditions under which anything in this lane may operate at all. A DAO in this domain would therefore need counsel and certification expertise before it needs a product roadmap, and its first written document would need to be a statement of which regulations apply and how the series will comply with them — not a vision statement. No such document exists today, and no regulator, certifier, or aviation partner has been named, because none is on record.

Within that constraint, the planned focus could cover several distinct lanes, and the honest thing to say is that none of them has been chosen. Flight-adjacent AI could mean decision support for pilots and controllers, where a human remains the decision-maker; maintenance and inspection support, where AI flags what humans then verify; airspace and traffic coordination, where the safety case is systemic rather than per-aircraft; or space systems, where the regulatory and operational picture is different again. Each lane has a different certification path, a different liability picture, and a different set of people whose lives depend on getting it right. Choosing a lane is the first real decision for this series, and it cannot be made by a name on a list. It would need a named human steward, advice from aviation safety experts, and a written scope approved at the Foundation's mission gate.

The relationship with Robotics DAO (Series 30) is the closest in the network, and it is worth stating what it is and is not. Both series concern physical AI that acts in the world, and both would need deployment standards, liability assignment, and the power to refuse a deployment that fails its checks. But aerospace adds certification and airspace to that list, and the failure modes are less forgiving: a ground robot can be stopped, while an aircraft often cannot. Any shared standards between the two series would need to be written as such, with the aerospace additions explicit, and no shared standards exist today. Similarly, any connection to Solar DAO (Series 32) — for example, energy systems for aerospace ground infrastructure — is a possibility to be explored, not a relationship that exists.

Validation for Aerospace DAO would be measured in certification and in trust, not in demonstrations. A flight video proves nothing about airworthiness; a certificate, a safety case, and an incident record do. The planned sequence is therefore longer and slower than for most series: written scope and regulatory map first, reviewed by counsel and by aviation safety experts; then a narrowly defined support role, with a human as the decision-maker and the AI as an assistant, operated under supervision; then a published review, including anything that went wrong; and only then any discussion of a wider role. Any proposal to move faster than that sequence — to put AI in a decision-making role in flight before the safety case is made and certified — would need to be refused at the mission gate. That refusal power is the point of having a governance series at all.

Governance: DAO-managed day to day, humans decide

Under the Foundation's intended venture-studio model, each W3 series is DAO-managed in its day-to-day operation: agents operate the series within limits set by humans, and a human Steward Council decides. That division is deliberate and non-negotiable in the model. Agents prepare proposals, analysis, drafts, and coordination; agents never sign legal documents, never move funds, never hire or fire, never offer securities, and never enact doctrine or charters. Humans execute. Every material decision — forming the series, opening a bank account, signing a partnership, spending money, or taking on an obligation — requires a named human's decision and signature.

The Foundation sits above the W3 series as a mission gate, not as an owner in the ordinary commercial sense: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head, and the Foundation's board must remain independent. Control in the intended model runs through written mission agreements and standards the series agree to, with counsel to design the exact structure. The Foundation's public-benefit purpose — safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI — is the standard every series is meant to be measured against, and the Windfall Clause framework is planned to apply across the W3 series: a legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits, under which returns above a threshold (to be set by counsel, expressed in the pitch framework as a percentage of global GDP) would flow to humanity through global public goods. The threshold, measurement, recipient, and enforceability are all blank for counsel today, and no series page states them as settled terms.

Governance of a series, once designated, would also need a written operating agreement, a named Steward Council, a compliance calendar, and separate accounts and records per series. None of that exists for this series yet, because the series itself does not exist yet as a legal entity. The governance described on this page is the intended model, not a description of a operating body. Anyone reading this page who wants to help shape that governance should contact the Foundation (see below) rather than assuming any of it is already in force.

Open questions this series must answer before it can proceed

  1. Which aerospace lane is in scope — pilot support, maintenance, airspace, or space — and which lanes are explicitly out of scope?
  2. Which regulations and certification paths apply, and has counsel confirmed the design before any operational claim is made?
  3. What safety case and incident process would govern any deployment, and who, by name, is accountable for it?

These are not rhetorical questions. Each one needs a written answer, approved by a named human, before the series passes its first stage gate. If you can help answer one of them, the contact below is the route.
