What the name suggests — and what it does not prove

A suggestive name is not a description, and this section is where that rule does its hardest work. The name points, if it points anywhere, at making — at forging something, and at doing so truly, or for what is true. That reading is name-implied only: it is a guess about intent drawn from a word, not a verified description of a product, and this page does not treat it as one. What a name sounds like is not evidence of what a venture does, and no page on this site treats it as evidence. The reason for the rule is practical, not pedantic: if this site described trueforge by elaborating on its name, a reader would leave believing the venture does the thing the name suggests, and would carry that belief into a conversation with a lawyer, a bank, an investor, or a potential partner. The belief would have been created by this site, out of nothing. A name-only page must therefore do something unusual for a website: it must describe the absence, and let the absence stand.

That does not mean the name is meaningless. A name is a choice, and the choice tells you what the people who wrote it were thinking about — the field they had in mind, the problem they wanted to be associated with, the company they wanted this venture to keep. What it does not tell you is whether the venture was ever built, whether anyone worked on it, whether it has a product, or whether it will ever exist in any form beyond the name. Names are cheap; companies are not. The gap between the two is the distance this page is measuring, and the measurement, at the time of writing, is the whole of the venture: a name, and nothing yet behind it.

There is also a second, subtler risk: writing a page so empty that a reader concludes the venture is a fiction, a mistake, or a deception. It is none of those things, on the evidence available. It is a name in planning materials for a network that is itself largely planned rather than formed. The honest response is not to delete the name, and not to decorate it, but to record it exactly as it stands — named, unbuilt, and waiting for the documents that would make it more than a name.

How it would be governed

Governance for trueforge, as planned, follows the model used across the intended W3 network. Day-to-day work would be agent-managed: agents would operate routine work within limits set in advance by humans. Humans decide, humans sign, and humans move funds. Agents do not sign documents, do not move money, do not hire or fire, do not offer securities, and do not enact doctrine or charters. A human Steward Council would hold decision authority, set the limits inside which agents may act, review what agents did, and remain accountable. That separation is deliberate: the point of an agent-managed venture is not to remove human responsibility, but to make routine work faster while keeping consequential decisions in human hands.

That model is itself planned, not operating for this project: no Steward Council roster, operating agreement, or published set of agent limits exists to point to today. The Foundation's role in the intended structure is as mission steward, under written mission agreements, with its board remaining independent: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. No agent message, draft, or page like this one is a commitment by the project or the Foundation; only a named human acting within signed, written authority can commit either one, and any future fundraising, partnership, or customer agreement would need its own documents, its own issuer, and counsel review.
