Fiduciary Corporate Shield

The Fiduciary Corporate Shield is the Foundation's central legal mechanism: a public benefit corporation's expanded business-judgment protection, designed so a board can prioritise safety over short-term profit and defend that choice in court. It is a mechanism and a framework, not a separate company — and it is not immunity. An earlier pitch version used the word “immune”; the legal documents corrected that to expanded business judgment, and this page follows the correction.

What this is

Every board that runs a company faces the same pressure: shareholders, or the threat of shareholders, demanding short-term profit, and a legal rule — the business judgment rule — that protects directors who act in good faith and with care, but that has historically been read as protecting decisions made for shareholder value. A board that slows a product launch for safety, refuses a profitable but risky deployment, or spends money on alignment work that does not pay off this quarter can find itself sued for doing the responsible thing. The Fiduciary Corporate Shield is the Foundation's answer to that pressure: write a public benefit purpose into the company's governing documents, so that a decision which serves that purpose is a decision within the board's mandate, and the business judgment rule protects it on those terms.

The mechanism has three parts, and they only work together: the form (a public benefit corporation whose charter states a public benefit purpose), the record (a board that documents its reasoning, so a decision can be seen to be deliberate and within the stated purpose), and the structure around it (the Open Global Investment model and the Windfall Clause, which give the decision its direction and its destination). None of the three is a company. The Shield is the legal layer of a company that would have to exist first.

Not immunity — the correction, stated plainly

The most important sentence on this page is a correction. An earlier pitch version of the Foundation's story described the Shield as making the board “immune” — immune from shareholder lawsuits when it prioritised safety over profit. That word was wrong, and the legal documents corrected it. The Shield is expanded business judgment, not immunity, and this page uses the corrected wording throughout, including in its title and its description.

Expanded business judgment means this: a board that acts in good faith, with the care of a prudent person, and within a stated public benefit purpose has a stronger defence against a claim that it breached its duties by favouring safety over short-term returns. The court still reviews whether the board acted in good faith, whether it was informed, whether it had a conflict of interest, and whether the decision was within the purpose the charter actually states. A board that acts in bad faith, is conflicted, fails to inform itself, or invokes “safety” as a cover for self-dealing gets no protection, and a board that is sued still has to defend the suit; the Shield improves its defence, it does not prevent the suit.

There is a second, quieter correction inside the first. The pitch version also implied that the Shield was already in force, protecting a board that already exists. It is not: the Shield depends on governing documents — articles stating the benefit purpose, bylaws setting out how benefit decisions are made and recorded, and a board that follows them — written by counsel for the specific entity. The filed nonprofit Foundation has articles with purpose language, but it is a nonprofit, and the Shield as pitched was designed for a for-profit benefit corporation. Until counsel chooses one track in writing and drafts the documents, the Shield is a designed mechanism, not a live defence.

How the mechanism is supposed to work

The mechanism works in four steps, each of which is a discipline as much as a legal rule. First, state the purpose. The articles of the company must name the public benefit — in the Foundation's architecture, safe, equitable, and socially beneficial AI, and the shared prosperity the Windfall Clause is meant to deliver — in language precise enough that a board decision can be tested against it. A purpose so vague that any decision fits it protects nothing, because a court cannot tell whether a decision served the purpose or merely claimed to. Drafting that language is counsel's work, and it is the work the whole mechanism stands on.

Second, decide in the open, on the record. When the board makes a decision that trades short-term profit for safety or benefit, it records what it considered: the risk, the evidence, the alternatives, the benefit served, and any dissent. The record is the Shield's working part, and the planned Governance Tracker — a live audit log of material proposals, fiduciary reviews, and compliance checks — is its public form. No live Tracker feed is claimed on this site until one is actually published and linked.

Third, keep governance and economics apart. The Open Global Investment model gives the global public economic participation through Class A shares with one vote, gives restricted aligned partners and sovereigns Class B shares with ten votes, and reserves Class C shares with 1,000 votes, control-only, to the Foundation — so the people who hold the economic upside are not the people who can vote the board into prioritising short-term returns over safety. No shares have been issued, no terms have been agreed, and no return is promised anywhere on this site.

Fourth, pre-commit the upside. The Windfall Clause is a legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits exist, that profits above a set threshold are redistributed globally — in the Foundation's planning, towards global public goods, healthcare access, energy infrastructure, and technological self-determination, in service of the UN Sustainable Development Goals. The threshold itself — a percentage of global GDP, with its measure, scope, share, and recipient — is blank for counsel to set. The Clause matters to the Shield because it removes the incentive to fight the benefit purpose in the first place. But a pre-commitment not yet written into governing documents is a plan, not a commitment.

What the Shield does not do

It does not make a board immune

It expands the business-judgment defence for decisions within a stated benefit purpose. Suits can still be filed, good faith and care are still reviewed, and bad faith or self-dealing gets no protection.

It does not replace governance

It needs a real board, real records, and a real benefit purpose in the articles. A name, a pitch slide, and a slogan do not create it.

It does not create a company

The Shield is a mechanism inside a company's governing documents. It is not a separate company, not a subsidiary, and not a series, and it has no valuation, no EIN, and no bank account.

It does not apply to the filed nonprofit as pitched

The pitch Shield was designed for a for-profit benefit corporation. The filed Foundation is a nonprofit with different duties. Counsel must choose one track in writing before the mechanism can be relied on.

One further limit belongs in the same passage. The Shield protects decisions; it does not make them good. A board can follow every procedural step and still make a wrong call about safety, and the Shield will not save the people affected. That is why the architecture pairs the Shield with the Alignment Evaluation Gate — pre-registered thresholds, an independent LLM judge, separation of powers, and a four-domain probe architecture, designed to refuse shipment of a model that fails its safety evaluation. Both are described as plans on this site, and neither is claimed as a live, operating system.

  • The correction itself: the legal documents' replacement of “immune” with expanded business judgment.

What is planned

  • Counsel's written choice of one track: filed nonprofit, or a new for-profit benefit corporation.
  • Governing documents that state the benefit purpose and the decision record — articles, bylaws, and a benefit report under the relevant statute.
  • A published Governance Tracker and Alignment Evaluation Gate, as live records rather than plans.
  • The Windfall Clause written into governing documents, with its threshold, measure, share, and recipient set — currently all blank for counsel.

Each step would need its own document, cited here with its date before this page could claim the Shield is in force. No date is given for any step, because no date is verified.

What would have to happen next

Before the Shield can be relied on, counsel would have to choose the track in writing (filed nonprofit, or a new for-profit benefit corporation — one form only), draft governing documents stating the benefit purpose and the decision record, seat a board that follows them and publish the first benefit report, and write the Windfall Clause into the same documents with its threshold, measure, share, and recipient set, with the amendment rule in the charter so the pre-commitment cannot be undone quietly later.

Until those steps are taken, the honest public record for the Fiduciary Corporate Shield is this page: a well-designed mechanism, a corrected description, and a defence that is not yet in force.

How it would be governed

Governance for the Fiduciary Corporate Shield, as planned, follows the model used across the intended W3 network. Day-to-day work would be agent-managed: agents would operate routine work within limits set in advance by humans. Humans decide, humans sign, and humans move funds. Agents do not sign documents, do not move money, do not hire or fire, do not offer securities, and do not enact doctrine or charters. A human Steward Council would hold decision authority, set the limits inside which agents may act, review what agents did, and remain accountable. That separation is deliberate: the point of an agent-managed venture is not to remove human responsibility, but to make routine work faster while keeping consequential decisions in human hands.

That model is itself planned, not operating for this project: no Steward Council roster, operating agreement, or published set of agent limits exists to point to today. The Foundation's role in the intended structure is as mission steward, under written mission agreements, with its board remaining independent: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. No agent message, draft, or page like this one is a commitment by the project or the Foundation; only a named human acting within signed, written authority can commit either one, and any future fundraising, partnership, or customer agreement would need its own documents, its own issuer, and counsel review.
