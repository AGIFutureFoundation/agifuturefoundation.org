WorldForge

WorldForge is the world-building side of the Foundation's network — the tooling and the catalogue of worlds that the planned metaverse presence would be built from. The numbers attached to it disagree with each other, and this page keeps the disagreement visible instead of picking the largest number: 80 worlds as stated on the Foundation site, and 544 worlds in the WorldForge-Browser repository's worlds file, every one of them with no RP1 attachment recorded.

What this is

WorldForge is the name for the world-building work in the Foundation's network: the creation of virtual worlds — spatial environments with terrain, structures, and assets — that the planned series and ventures would inhabit. On the live Foundation site it appears as part of the network story, alongside the series and the RP1 spatial-computing stack. What is verified about it is narrow, and this page stays inside that narrow verified core: there is a public repository called WorldForge-Browser, it contains a worlds file listing 544 worlds, and every one of those 544 entries records no RP1 attachment. There is also site copy stating 80 worlds. Those two numbers describe different things — a site claim and a repository file — and neither of them is a count of worlds that exist as finished, rendering, inhabited places.

That distinction matters because “worlds” can mean several different things, and the Foundation's materials use the word loosely. A world can be an entry in a JSON catalogue, a fabric definition file published on GitHub Pages, a fully rendered environment in a browser like Artemis, or a live place attached to RP1 that people can enter. Those are four different stages, and a world can be at stage one without being at stage four. The six anchored fabrics in the Foundation's network — Cognition, Empathic, Diagnostic, Locator, SmartCiti Holodeck, and Biota — are at stage two: their fabric JSON files are published on GitHub Pages, but the roughly 200 GLB assets and the map.wasm file they reference are not hosted, so they do not render fully in Artemis yet. Zero worlds are live on RP1, which is stage four. WorldForge, as a name, covers the tooling that would move a world from one stage to the next; it does not by itself prove that any world has completed the journey.

The numbers, kept apart

Three numbers circulate for WorldForge and the worlds around it, and they must not be blended into a single claim. Each is quoted below with its source, and none is quoted as a count of finished, rendering, inhabited worlds.

80 worlds, as stated on the Foundation site. The live site copy states 80 worlds for WorldForge. That figure is quoted here as site copy, not as a verified fact. No document reviewed for this site explains how the 80 was counted — whether it is worlds built, worlds listed, worlds planned, or worlds in some other sense — and no independent count confirms it. Until a source explains the counting method, the honest way to quote it is exactly this: “80 worlds, as stated on the Foundation site.” It should not be quoted to a lawyer, a bank, or an investor as a verified count, and it should not be combined with any other number below.

544 worlds in the WorldForge-Browser repository. The public WorldForge-Browser repository contains a worlds file listing 544 worlds. That is a verified count of entries in a file, and it is the largest number in this section — which is precisely why it must not be quoted as “544 worlds built”. An entry in a catalogue is not a finished world. What the file does verify, and what matters more than its length, is the attachment column: every one of the 544 entries records rp1 as null. Not one of the 544 is recorded as attached to RP1. That is consistent with the wider verified fact that zero worlds are live on RP1, and it is the single most important thing the repository file establishes.

41 worlds, and the two repositories. The WorldForge-Browser repository is public and holds 41 worlds in one verified count, while a separate private repository, agi-corp-worlds, holds 40 worlds. Those counts describe repositories, not the same catalogue as the 544-entry worlds file, and they must not be mixed with it or with each other. The first 41 worlds in the WorldForge set also carry latitude and longitude values that differ from the agi-corp anchor coordinates.

34 worlds, and 35 cells. Other Foundation materials have mentioned 34 worlds and 35 cells. Those figures disagree with both the 80 and the 544, and with the 33 named series in the April 2026 internal list. This page does not resolve the disagreement by choosing a favourite. The safe counts on this site are the ones with a cited source: 33 named series in the April 2026 internal list, and 544 entries in the WorldForge-Browser worlds file, all with no RP1 attachment.

What is verified vs what is only a name

Verified

  • A public repository named WorldForge-Browser exists.
  • Its worlds file lists 544 worlds, all with rp1: null.
  • Six anchored fabric JSONs are published on GitHub Pages (Cognition, Empathic, Diagnostic, Locator, SmartCiti Holodeck, Biota).
  • Zero worlds are live on RP1.

Only a name, or site copy

  • “80 worlds” is site copy, with no counting method verified.
  • No finished, fully rendering world is verified.
  • No users, visitors, revenue, customers, or partners are verified for WorldForge.
  • No team, timeline, or launch date is verified.

The gap between those two columns is the whole story of this page. WorldForge is real as a repository and a catalogue, and it is unverified as a finished product with users. The work that has been done is cataloguing and fabric-writing; the work that has not been done is hosting the assets, verifying a full render, and attaching a world to RP1.

How it fits

Tier. WorldForge is infrastructure, not a company and not a series. In the intended three-tier structure it would sit underneath the commercial tier: the tooling that builds the worlds the series would inhabit, rather than a cell inside the W3 network. It is therefore not assigned a series number, not presented as a subsidiary, and not given a valuation. Suggested valuations in the Foundation's planning papers cover series and proposed corporations; no suggested valuation is assigned to WorldForge on this page, and none should be invented for it.

Relationship to RP1. WorldForge builds worlds; RP1 is the spatial-computing infrastructure those worlds would run on, and Artemis is RP1's native browser. The relationship is sequential, not simultaneous: a world must be built, its assets hosted, and its render verified in Artemis before any RP1 attach can even be attempted, and the attach itself is authorised world by world. No fabric or service has ever been attached for AGI Corp, and no world in the network has been verified live on RP1. The RP1 ecosystem page gives the full verified account of that stack, including the components that are only names on the live site.

Relationship to the series. The six anchored fabrics map to series where a mapping exists: Cognition to S01, Empathic to S02, Diagnostic to S03, Locator to S08, and the SmartCiti Holodeck to S10 SmartCiti.X. Biota is the exception that proves the rule about not inventing mappings: its anchored world sits at -38.713, -27.248, but Biota has no series number, and this site does not assign it one. The remaining 28 series have no anchored fabric verified, and their pages say so. WorldForge's catalogue is therefore much larger than its anchored set, and the anchored set is much larger than anything live — a nesting of claims that collapses if the stages are confused.

What exists today vs what is planned

What exists today

  • The WorldForge-Browser public repository and its 544-entry worlds file, all rp1: null.
  • Six anchored fabric JSONs on GitHub Pages, with their coordinates verified.
  • The Foundation's filing record (California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation, Entity No. B20260196664, filed 2026-04-24, EIN assigned 2026-05-07).
  • This page, as a public statement of the numbers and their limits.

What is planned

  • Host the roughly 200 GLB assets and map.wasm the anchored fabrics reference, and verify a full render in Artemis, with the date and result recorded.
  • Explain or correct the 80-world site figure with a stated counting method, or withdraw it.
  • Reconcile the 544, 41, 40, 34, and 35 counts in one dated note that says what each counts.
  • Pursue an RP1 attach world by world, only after a render is verified — and report the first live world with evidence, not assertion.

Each step would need its own document or dated verification. None is presented here as done, scheduled, or promised by a date, because no date is verified. In particular, no timeline is given for a first live world on RP1: “authorised world by world” is a permission, not a schedule.

What would have to happen next

Before WorldForge can be described as anything more than a catalogue and a set of fabric files, four things would have to happen, in order. First, the assets: the roughly 200 GLB files and map.wasm referenced by the six anchored fabrics would have to be hosted where the fabric files can reach them, and the hosting location would have to be recorded. Second, the render: a full render in Artemis would have to be verified and dated, with a screenshot or a log, not merely asserted. Third, the count: the Foundation would have to publish one dated note reconciling the 80, 544, 41, 40, 34, and 35 figures, stating what each counts and which of them, if any, is the number to quote. Fourth, the attach: a single world would have to be attached to RP1 and verified live, with the evidence published, before any claim of a live world could be made — and that first live world would be one world, not proof that the whole catalogue is live.

Until those steps are taken, the honest public record for WorldForge is this page: a real repository, a real catalogue of 544 entries with no RP1 attachment, six anchored fabrics that do not yet render fully, a site figure of 80 worlds quoted as site copy, and zero worlds live on RP1.

How it would be governed

Governance for WorldForge, as planned, follows the model used across the intended W3 network. Day-to-day work would be agent-managed: agents would operate routine work within limits set in advance by humans. Humans decide, humans sign, and humans move funds. Agents do not sign documents, do not move money, do not hire or fire, do not offer securities, and do not enact doctrine or charters. A human Steward Council would hold decision authority, set the limits inside which agents may act, review what agents did, and remain accountable. That separation is deliberate: the point of an agent-managed venture is not to remove human responsibility, but to make routine work faster while keeping consequential decisions in human hands.

That model is itself planned, not operating for this project: no Steward Council roster, operating agreement, or published set of agent limits exists to point to today. The Foundation's role in the intended structure is as mission steward, under written mission agreements, with its board remaining independent: a California nonprofit cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head. No agent message, draft, or page like this one is a commitment by the project or the Foundation; only a named human acting within signed, written authority can commit either one, and any future fundraising, partnership, or customer agreement would need its own documents, its own issuer, and counsel review.
