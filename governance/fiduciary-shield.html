The Fiduciary Shield

California benefit-corporation status expands the business-judgment rule, so a board that weighs safety and public benefit alongside shareholder returns is not left exposed for having done so. The Shield moves the litigation boundary. It is a legal protection for a particular kind of decision — not a wall around the company, and not immunity.

Read this first: the Shield is NOT immunity.
The pitch deck's Slide 6 described the board as "legally immune to shareholder lawsuits" when sacrificing short-term profit for AI safety guardrails. That claim overstates California law and was corrected in the legal documents: the Shield is an expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, subject to the duties in California Corporations Code §§14620–14621 and to the Articles. A benefit corporation can still be sued, can still be acquired, and can still have its benefit purpose amended by the required vote. Nothing on this page says "immune", and no document built from this page should either.

What the Shield is

The idea is simple to state and hard to live by. In a traditional for-profit company, directors are judged above all on shareholder wealth: a decision that sacrifices near-term profit for safety invites a shareholder lawsuit, and the fear of that lawsuit pushes safety to lose every race against speed. The Shield is the legal answer to that incentive: California public benefit corporation (PBC) status, which rewrites the board's duties so that safety and public benefit are part of the job, not a deviation from it.

Under the pitch standards translated into draft legal structure, the Shield has three working parts. First, the Articles carry a stated benefit purpose — in draft form, advancing safe, equitable, and socially beneficial artificial intelligence and the global distribution of its benefits — which counsel must draft in final form. Second, directors must consider that benefit purpose alongside shareholder interests, under California Corporations Code §§14620–14621, as counsel confirms. That duty is what lets a board reject a buy-out that threatens safety on benefit grounds, rather than being compelled to sell to the highest bidder. Third, the board reports on benefit performance annually, under the §14630 benefit report, so the public-benefit side of the bargain is accounted for in public, not merely asserted. The pitch deck calls this a "two-way check": accountable for both profit and public benefit, where a traditional company answers to a one-way ratchet for profit.

What the Shield protects, in the corrected legal language, is this: "expanded business-judgment protection for decisions that consider the benefit purpose in good faith, subject to §§14620–14621." A board that considers the benefit purpose in good faith — weighing safety and public benefit alongside returns, documenting that reasoning, and acting within its duties — receives the expanded protection of the business-judgment rule when a profit-focused shareholder challenges the decision. The protection attaches to the decision-making, not to the outcome, and it does not remove the duties themselves. A board that ignores the benefit purpose, or acts in bad faith, is not shielded by it. That is the practical meaning of "moving the litigation boundary" — the line at which a shareholder can credibly sue over a safety-first decision moves, because the law itself now asks the board to consider the benefit the decision protects.

What the Shield is not

The correction matters enough to say twice, in full. The pitch deck (15 slides, Part 3, "The Wealth of a New World") states on Slide 6 that "by expanding the Business Judgment Rule, the Foundation's board becomes legally immune to shareholder lawsuits when sacrificing short-term commercial profits to implement strict AI safety guardrails." The legal documents built from that deck flag this as an overstatement of California law, and correct it: a California benefit corporation's directors must consider the public benefit (§14620) and are protected by an expanded business-judgment analysis, but they are not immune from shareholder suits, and a PBC can still be sued, acquired, or have its benefit purpose amended by the required vote. The paperwork is explicit: do not copy Slide 6's immunity claim into an offering document, into Articles, or into an investor letter without counsel rewriting it.

The Shield is also not a substitute for the rest of the governance architecture. It protects a board's benefit-considering decisions; it does not, by itself, designate a series, seat a Steward Council, adopt a Windfall Clause in any operating agreement, screen a sovereign shareholder, or open a bank account. Each of those is a separate step, with its own documents and its own open items.

How the Shield fits the rest of the architecture

The pitch deck presents three interlocking gears: the PBC Shield (legal protection for the safety mission), the Windfall Clause (the distributive engine for global wealth), and the OGI Shares (economic inclusion to defuse conflict). The Shield is the first gear. It does not distribute anything by itself; it makes the other two gears governable, by protecting the board that must hold the line on safety while the other gears move money and ownership.

Open Global Investment

The broad-ownership model: economic upside open widely, governance held with the Foundation. The Shield is what lets the board keep safety decisions out of a hostile takeover — "everyone can own the future, but the future's safety cannot be subjected to a hostile takeover." Class A shares (1 vote) are the broad-access, fully economic class; Class B shares (10 votes) are restricted to aligned partners and sovereign entities meeting strict safety and geopolitical frameworks, with sanctions, export-control, and CFIUS screening where applicable. Share classes

Three classes, as the pitch standard requires: Class A, 1 vote, full economic participation, global public access; Class B, 10 votes, economic plus voting, restricted and screened; Class C, 1,000 votes, held exclusively by the Foundation, control only, no economic participation. Class C is the mechanism of the Foundation's "absolute, unshakeable ethical control" — and that control must be disclosed to Class A and B investors as a governance fact. The Shield and Class C do different jobs: the Shield protects benefit-considering decisions; Class C controls who makes them. Share Structure →

Steward Council

At the series level, each series' Steward Council is the mission gate: three consenting humans, with agents operating day-to-day within human-set limits and humans deciding. The Shield operates at the board level; the Council operates at the series level. Neither replaces the other, and the Foundation's control over the series is exercised through mission and voting agreements and the Council gate — not by the Foundation owning each series' economics. The PBC fork — read before relying on this page

There are two different things called "AGI Future Foundation PBC" in the family's materials, and they are not the same entity type. As filed and verified, the Foundation is a California Nonprofit Public Benefit Corporation, Entity No. B20260196664, Articles filed 2026-04-24, with SOS approval 2026-04-30, principal and mailing address 995 Market St AGI Lab -10, San Francisco, CA 94103, agent Mike Rice, and an EIN assigned 2026-05-07. Its purpose carries 501(c)(3) language, with irrevocable dedication and no private inurement. A nonprofit with this filing cannot sell equity, SAFEs, profit shares, or tokens, and cannot distribute profits.

As labelled on the live website — including on this site's home page, which calls the Foundation a "California Public Benefit Corporation" and describes the Shield as a for-profit benefit-corporation defense — "PBC" is used in the for-profit sense: a benefit corporation with dual-class equity, Class A profit participation, and Open Global Investment. The corporate raise advice follows the filed status, because a court, the Attorney General, the IRS, and an investor's counsel will follow the filing, not the website label. If a for-profit benefit corporation is intended, that is a different corporation to form, or an amendment or conversion that counsel must advise on — a nonprofit cannot simply "become" a for-profit and keep its donations.

The fork is unresolved. The review records it as the single most dangerous confusion in the pack if a reader mixes the two stories, and as the first of its questions for counsel: which issuer track should be chosen — a new for-profit PBC, a conversion, or AGI Corp instead. Until counsel chooses one track in writing, no solicitation should use the for-profit figures, and the Shield as described on this page must be understood as the for-profit PBC track's design, not as a protection already held by the filed nonprofit. The raise advice is explicit that the website's Class A, dual-class, and Open Global Investment copy describes a for-profit equity structure that does not apply to the filed nonprofit, and that using it to solicit money for the Foundation risks both a securities violation and a charitable-solicitation violation.

Two facts stay attached to this page. First, no IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter is in the files for the Foundation — the EIN notice lists Form 1120 — so the Foundation must never be presented as IRS tax-exempt, and no donation may be promised as tax-deductible; the correct language, if true, is "501(c)(3) determination pending". Second, the Foundation's Statement of Information (initially due around 2026-07-23) and Attorney General Registry registration are not evidenced in the files, and the review records them as apparently past and unverified as of 2026-11-03 — status to be checked, not assumed. The Shield does not cure any of that.
