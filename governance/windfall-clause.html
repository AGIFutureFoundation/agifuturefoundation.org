The Windfall Clause: sharing excess AGI profits with humanity

The Windfall Clause is a legal pre-commitment, hardwired before profits are generated: once profits pass a set threshold, the excess is shared with humanity instead of flowing to shareholders as ordinary profit. This page explains the clause as designed — and what does not exist yet.

What the Windfall Clause is

The idea is simple: voluntary corporate philanthropy often fails under immense market pressure. A board that means to share profits later can be sued, outvoted, acquired, or worn down before the profits arrive. The Windfall Clause answers that by making the sharing decision before the profits exist — hardwired into the governing documents, so sharing is the default rather than a decision re-argued at the moment of success.

As designed, the clause obligates all 33 W3 series — the profit-generating series in the April 2026 internal list — to share a portion of excess AGI profits toward international benefit sharing once a specific economic threshold is met. A clause living only in one company's articles, with no obligation at the level of the series that generate the profits, would not meet the design standard. The obligation is meant to run to the series themselves, and to their successors, to the fullest extent permitted by law.

Two guardrails are built in. First, the clause must be adopted before a series becomes profitable: one adopted after a series is already profitable, or set aside by a simple board majority when profits arrive, does not meet the standard. Second, counsel must confirm in writing whether the clause survives a sale, merger, or change of control. Survival must not be assumed.

The threshold: a percentage of Global GDP

The threshold is expressed as a percentage of Global GDP, not as a fixed dollar amount: a dollar figure set today would be overtaken by the scale of the economy it measures, while a share of global output scales with the world it is drawn from. But a percentage of Global GDP is a concept, not a calculable trigger, until four things are defined — and none of them has been defined yet.

First, the percentage itself is blank: no threshold number exists today, and none in the pitch deck or the drafts may be quoted as the threshold. Second, the GDP measure is blank — which source (for example the World Bank or the IMF), which year, and which vintage. Third, the profit measure is blank — which profits count, for example audited net income, operating profit, or distributable profit, after tax and after any research-and-development reserve. Fourth, the scope is blank — each series separately, the W3 series in aggregate, or the parent plus the W3 series in aggregate.

Until those four items are filled in, the threshold is not calculable, and the clause must not be described as "triggered at $X." A percentage of Global GDP with no percentage, no measure, and no scope is a direction of travel, not a trigger. The design provides for an independent auditor to calculate it from audited accounts and publish it, with the measures stated so a reader can recompute the trigger; the auditor's role and the dispute forum are themselves blanks for counsel.

Below the threshold, and above it

The clause draws one line, and the treatment of profits changes on either side of it; the threshold is shown as a blank, because that is what it is today.

Below the threshold: standard returns and research

Profits up to the threshold reward early investors and sustain research and development — the design's own language. The clause is not a cap on ordinary success: below the threshold, profits follow the normal distribution rules, distributed to the holders of the economic shares and retained for research, exactly as they would without the clause.

Above the threshold: windfall profits belong to humanity

Profits above the threshold are, in the pitch deck's words, profits that "belong to humanity" — in legal terms, Windfall Profits, distributed under the clause rather than retained as ordinary shareholder profit or paid out as ordinary dividends. The pitch deck calls the share going to humanity "a massive portion" — but it gives no number, and none may be attributed to the pitch. The Windfall Share is a blank for the board and counsel to set, and counsel must confirm that the Windfall Share plus any remainder adds to one hundred percent of excess profits, with no gap.

Once the share is set, the Windfall Profits would be paid to the Windfall Administrator within a number of days of the audited accounts for that period — the number of days is itself blank — for distribution to four Benefit Buckets: Global Public Goods, Healthcare Access, Energy Infrastructure, and Technological Self-Determination. The buckets share equally unless the board and counsel set different shares, and the money in each case is for projects advancing the UN Sustainable Development Goals, directly funded under criteria the Administrator publishes.

Two limits apply to every windfall distribution: none may go to a shareholder, director, officer, or series manager as a return on their shares or interests, and none may be used to buy control, influence a government, or fund a political campaign. Counsel must confirm each bucket's and recipient's charitable or public-benefit eligibility; those confirmations have not been obtained.

Who administers the windfall

Windfall Profits are administered by an Administrator not yet chosen: the Foundation, a separate Windfall Fund, or a trust — counsel to advise on tax and charitable status. If the Administrator is the nonprofit Foundation, the money must be held in a segregated Windfall account, reported separately from Foundation donations, and used only for the Benefit Buckets — not as general Foundation funds, and not to fund the parent company, a series, or any SAFE round. The Administrator would publish an annual public Windfall report covering the threshold calculation, the excess profits, the share paid, the bucket allocations, the recipients, and the audit. That report has no author yet: there is no Administrator, no threshold, and no windfall to report.

Amendment: supermajority plus Class C

A pre-commitment a simple majority could repeal at the moment it matters would be no commitment at all, so the clause carries its own amendment protection: it may be amended, suspended, or repealed only by a supermajority vote — plus the Class C vote — with the exact percentage still blank for counsel to set. The design allows amendment in a genuine emergency, but not by a simple board majority when profits arrive. An amendment reducing the Windfall Share, raising the threshold, or removing a Benefit Bucket, adopted without that vote, would be void.

Class C is the Foundation-only control class: 1,000 votes per share, held exclusively by the Foundation, with no economic participation — control only. Its weight is part of the amendment protection, and it must be disclosed to Class A and Class B investors as a governance fact: they own economics, they do not control safety decisions, and Class C's control is not a guarantee that the clause will never change. The supermajority percentages — for Class C, and for Class A and Class B voting as separate classes — are blanks for counsel to set. For series instruments, the same protection is mapped across as amendment only by a supermajority plus the Foundation's mission-control vote, with counsel to draft the mapping expressly.

How the clause fits the share structure

The Windfall Clause operates inside the Open Global Investment model, and the share structure is what makes the two fit together. Class A carries one vote per share and full economic participation, and is the broad-access class, open globally subject to securities law in each jurisdiction. Class B carries ten votes per share, is both economic and voting, but restricted to aligned partners and sovereign entities meeting strict safety and geopolitical frameworks, subject to sanctions, export-control, and national-security screening. Class C carries 1,000 votes per share, is held exclusively by the Foundation, and has no economic participation at all.

The point of separating economics from control is that everyone can own the future while the future's safety cannot be subjected to a hostile takeover — the pitch deck's framing, and the reason Class C exists. But the design documents add a disclosure duty the pitch does not: Class C's control must be disclosed to Class A and Class B investors as a governance risk, not hidden behind decoupling language. Control is not ownership: the Foundation's control runs through Class C at the parent level, mission and voting agreements, and each series' Steward Council mission gate — not through owning the series' economics or profits. The clause reduces the profits available to shareholders above the threshold, by design, and any offering document mentioning it must disclose that cost, the blanks as they stand, Class C control, and sovereign-participation screening. Participation in the upside, where the pitch deck speaks of "guaranteed participation," means participation if there is upside: not a guaranteed return, and no guarantee that any profit will ever exceed the threshold.

Pitch illustrations, labelled as such

Two figures from the pitch deck are sometimes quoted alongside the Windfall Clause, and both need the same label: pitch illustration, not verified data. The first is the comparison of global wealth distribution under the Open Global Investment model, given as a Gini coefficient of approximately 0.89 against approximately 0.96 for a United States-only nationalized AGI program — pitch illustrations, not sourced statistics, not to be quoted as measured data or forecast. The second is the phrase "a massive portion" for the Windfall Share: pitch language for a share that has no number, never to be presented as a percentage. The same labelling applies to the deck's statement that the board becomes "legally immune" to shareholder lawsuits when sacrificing short-term profit for safety, which overstates California law; the legal documents correct it to expanded business-judgment protection for benefit-considering decisions, subject to the California Corporations Code. This page does not repeat the immunity claim, and nothing here is a legal conclusion about what a court would hold.
