Governance · AGI Future Foundation

The Steward Council: the human gate

In the Foundation's corporate design, agents run the day-to-day work and draft the proposals. Humans decide. The Steward Council is the human backstop: the only authority that can enact, decline, or amend a charter, or move funds. No agent proposal, token vote, or automated instruction is a corporate act on its own.

What the Steward Council is

The Steward Council is the human decision-making body for each operating series in the family design — the W3 LLC Wyoming series and the Delaware cohort series (C1S1, C1S2, C1S3, and so on). Each series is managed under a DAO structure for day-to-day operations, but the DAO is governance, not a legal person: a DAO vote does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities. Only the series' legal manager or Steward Council, with signed authority, does that.

The design in the corporate files is specific about the council's role and deliberately narrow about who holds it:

  • Enacts, declines, or amends charters. The council is the only authority that can enact a charter change, decline one, or amend a charter.
  • Moves funds. The council is the only authority that can move a series' funds. Until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council — and a series without an effective council is not operating, whatever its status label says.
  • Decides budgets, major contracts, and pivot-or-kill calls. At each quarterly review, the council decides budget, hiring, major contracts, fundraising, and charter changes for its series, and recommends pivot or kill where the studio model gives it that role.
  • Is human, named, and consenting. Stewards must be named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials before any council action is treated as effective. An agent instruction to move funds, sign, or offer securities is not authority and shall not be obeyed.

A boundary the files draw plainly: the 3-seat, 2-of-3 signed-vote design described in the corporate files is the design for each series Steward Council. The Foundation itself is governed by its own board of directors, described below. The files do not specify a Foundation-level Steward Council with a seat count or voting rule — counsel and the board are to decide that. Likewise, the AGI Fellowship church is a separate entity with its own governance; its council design is not the Foundation's, and nothing about it is imported here.

The proposal flow: draft, decide, execute

Every substantive act in the family design passes through the same three-step flow. Agents work in step one. Humans own steps two and three. The human gate is not optional, and it is not a formality — a project that fails a gate is paused or killed, not carried forward on agent momentum.

The same flow governs the venture studio's stage gates: an agent brief goes to human selection and a Foundation mission-fit screen (Gate 0); agent research goes to a human problem/solution decision (Gate 1); an agent build goes to human technical and legal review (Gate 2); launch requires human approval of claims, pricing, and contracts (Gate 3); operation and scale run through Steward Council budget decisions and quarterly review (Gate 4); exit or wind-down is a board and Council decision, with IP, data, funds, and customer obligations resolved in writing before closure (Gate 5). Killing a project is treated in the design as a studio success, not a failure to hide.

The eight agent roles — and their limits

Each series' DAO charter is to name which agent holds which role, who the human coordinator or manager is, and what each agent may and may not do. The eight roles in the family design are:

1 · Research synthesis

Scan research, markets, repositories, and community input; draft project briefs and rank them against mission and market criteria. A human founder or steward selects which ideas enter validation — no idea becomes a series on agent ranking alone.

2 · Task routing

Route work across the series' agent team and keep the queue moving within the written limits humans set.

3 · Proposal drafting

Draft proposals for the Steward Council to decide on. A proposal is a draft until a human adopts it; an agent proposal must never be presented as a human decision.

4 · Coordination

Coordinate day-to-day work, scheduling, outreach, and records under human board or minister direction.

5 · Progress tracking

Track stage gates, KPIs, and portfolio dashboard reporting, and flag risks for human review.

6 · Monitoring

Monitor operations, compliance checklists, and mission-fit standards, and surface what needs a human decision.

7 · Collaborator scouting

Find potential collaborators, partners, and community input, and draft the approach for human approval.

8 · Grant drafting

Draft grant applications and diligence packs. Humans pitch, humans authorize, and counsel reviews offering materials. Agents do not solicit investment.

A coordinator covers any unfilled role. Agent identity is recorded — agent name, model, and instance per role — so a proposal can be traced to the agent that drafted it and the human that adopted it. If an agent or model changes, the charter's role assignment is updated and minuted. Roles are functions, not persons: a new agent does not inherit a human's signing authority. The default spending limit for agents is $0 without Council approval, unless the Council sets a written threshold — and the default is $0. Agents hold no equity, no tokens, and no profit interests; they are tools and operators, not owners.

The Foundation's own governance: an independent board

The Steward Council governs the series. The Foundation itself is governed by its board of directors — and the files are emphatic that the board must stay independent. A California nonprofit public benefit corporation cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head corporation the way a subsidiary is. Its board owes fiduciary duties to the charitable mission and the public, not to any other entity's shareholders. In the intended structure, AGI Corp sits at the top commercially as the head family corporation, while the Foundation acts as mission steward and manager — but its authority comes from written management and mission agreements, not from ownership by AGI Corp.

That independence has practical consequences the files spell out:

  • The Foundation can refuse. The board must retain the right to refuse a project that fails mission standards, even if AGI Corp wants it. If counsel cannot design the control mechanism cleanly — a mission-lock agreement, a veto over mission changes, independent directors — the head must be restructured, with AGI Corp as family office and the Foundation as independent steward, and agreements between equals rather than parent and child.
  • The Foundation is the mission gate. Every studio project passes a Foundation mission-fit review before it receives a series number, and the Foundation board can withdraw mission approval for a project. That project then loses Foundation support and Foundation branding, even if AGI Corp wants to continue it commercially.
  • Money stays in its own lane. The Foundation takes donations and grants only, in its own account, for charitable purposes. It may charge a documented management fee only if counsel confirms it is fair, reasonable, and consistent with exempt status, and only under a written agreement approved by disinterested directors. Foundation money is never a SAFE, never studio equity, and never a subsidy for a commercial series' losses, unless counsel advises a specific lawful structure in writing. No SAFE, equity, profit share, or return may be issued by the filed nonprofit Foundation.
  • Control runs through paper, not hierarchy. If AGI Corp manages a series, or the Foundation manages a company to mission standards, the fee, scope, term, and termination are in a signed management agreement, approved by disinterested directors of the managed entity, at a documented fair-market rate. No handshake management.

The draft bylaws describe the board's mechanics, and they are drafts — not adopted, not filed. As drafted, the board would consist of three or five directors (a number the board must choose), serving two-year terms, with a quorum of a majority of directors then in office and action by a majority of directors present at a duly held meeting; action without a meeting would require unanimous written consent. The corporation would have no members: all corporate powers are exercised by the board. Directors serve without compensation, except reasonable documented reimbursement. The draft names Mike Rice as an initial director and co-founder, and Russell Vacanti as co-founder — with consent pending, meaning Russell Vacanti must confirm in writing that he accepts the role before the bylaws are adopted with his name in them — and a third director still to be recruited. No other council or board members are named in the files, and none are named on this page.

Conflicts, records, and the compliance calendar

Governance in the files is as much a record-keeping discipline as a decision-making one. The inter-entity and conflict-of-interest policy — itself a draft, to be adopted separately by each board — requires that any director, officer, employee, or key person with a financial interest in a transaction disclose it in writing before discussion, including the other entity, the nature and size of the interest, and the price or terms if known, with the disclosure recorded in the minutes. The interested person leaves the meeting for discussion and vote, except to answer factual questions if invited. Approval requires a majority of disinterested directors, who must minute that the transaction is fair, reasonable, and in the entity's best interest, that comparable arm's-length alternatives were considered (or why none were available), and the basis for the price or terms.

The policy also draws hard lines: no loan from a nonprofit to a director, officer, or AGI Corp except as California law expressly permits and disinterested directors approve; no sale, license, or transfer of nonprofit assets below fair market value, and no nonprofit IP transferred without a signed assignment or license at fair market value; no commingling — no shared bank account, and no paying one entity's expenses from another's account without a written cost-sharing or service agreement allocating costs on a documented basis; and no nonprofit guarantee of a SAFE, loan, or obligation of AGI Corp or any series cell. Donor and grant money to a nonprofit must not be described as investment in AGI Corp, and investor money to AGI Corp must not be described as a charitable donation. Every solicitation and receipt names the receiving entity exactly, with its SOS name.

The compliance calendar and governance tracker turn that discipline into a routine. For the Foundation, the calendar as drafted includes the CA SOS Statement of Information (initially due within 90 days of filing, then biennially — counsel to confirm the period), CA Attorney General Registry of Charities registration and annual RRF-1, the IRS return (per the EIN notice, Form 1120 due 2027-08-15, with the CPA to confirm the correct return after any determination), follow-up on the 501(c)(3) determination until a letter is received, San Francisco business registration renewal, monthly Mercury and ledger reconciliation, board meetings and minutes at least annually and as the bylaws require, conflict disclosures annually and on any new transaction, and an annual insurance review — D&O and general liability, with the decision minuted. The governance tracker records one row per meeting or consent: date, entity, meeting or consent type, directors or managers present, resolutions passed, related-party disclosures and recusals, and whether minutes were filed. Its rules are short: no meeting without minutes; no related-party transaction without a disclosure and recusal row; and a filing is not "done" until proof — an SOS confirmation, AG receipt, IRS acknowledgement, or bank statement — is filed in that entity's pack. An agent's "submitted" flag is not proof.

What is not yet decided

This page describes a design, not a finished institution. The files are explicit about what remains open, and this section keeps those gaps visible rather than filling them:

  • No Foundation-level council specification. The files do not specify the Foundation council's seat count or voting rule — counsel and the board are to decide. The 3-seat, 2-of-3 design in the files belongs to each series' Steward Council, not to the Foundation board.
  • No named stewards. No Steward Council members are confirmed in the files, and none are named here. Until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council.
  • Drafts, not adoptions. The Foundation bylaws, board consent, and inter-entity conflict policy are drafts. The bylaws' board size is a bracketed choice (three or five), the third director is still to be recruited, and Russell Vacanti's co-founder and director role is consent pending.
  • Filings still to verify. No IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter is in the files for the Foundation, and the Statement of Information and CA Attorney General registration are not evidenced in the uploads. Their status is unverified — not assumed filed, not assumed unfiled.
  • Structure is intended, not filed. The Wyoming AGI Corp head, the W3 LLC Wyoming series company, and the Delaware cohort series company are intended designs, not verified filings. The Foundation's independence rules above are the design counsel must make work — they are not a claim that the structure is already in place.
  • Economics are blank. Studio ownership in a series, founder and team equity, management fees, SAFE terms, revenue sharing, Windfall terms, and Open Global Investment terms are all blanks for counsel and the board to set in signed agreements. A term that is not in a signed agreement does not exist.
