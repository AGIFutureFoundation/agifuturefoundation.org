Stewardship: the human gate

In the Foundation's design, agents run the day-to-day work and draft the proposals. Humans decide. The Steward Council is the human backstop: the only authority that can enact, decline, or amend a charter, or move funds. A steward is not an advisor or a title on a website. A steward is a named, consenting human holding real signing authority — and being accountable for using it.

What a Steward Council is

A Steward Council is the human decision-making body for each operating series in the family design — the W3 LLC Wyoming series and the Delaware cohort series (C1S1, C1S2, C1S3, and so on). Each series is managed under a DAO structure for day-to-day operations, but the DAO is governance, not a legal person: a DAO vote does not sign a contract, move funds, or issue securities. Only the series' legal manager or Steward Council, with signed authority, does that. Stewards must be named, consenting humans holding real keys and signing credentials before any council action is treated as effective.

The design for each series council is three seats with 2-of-3 signed votes. That number belongs to the series councils, not to the Foundation's board, and the files do not specify a Foundation-level Steward Council with a seat count or voting rule — counsel and the board are to decide that. A series council decides for its series, inside the mission standards the Foundation sets. The Foundation's board governs the Foundation itself, owes fiduciary duties to the charitable mission and the public, and must stay independent — it cannot be owned or controlled by a for-profit head corporation the way a subsidiary is.

Until stewards are named, consenting, and holding keys, a series has no effective council — and a series without an effective council is not operating, whatever its status label says. The council is not a garnish on a project that is already running; it is the thing that makes the project able to act at all. A series with agents, a charter draft, and no council can propose, but it cannot decide, cannot sign, and cannot move funds.

What stewards decide

Stewards decide the things that cannot be delegated to software, to a vote, or to momentum. The files give the council four kinds of authority.

Charters

Enact, decline, or amend a charter. A charter is the written statement of what a series is for, what it may do, and what it may not do — and the council is the only authority that can change it. An agent may draft a charter change; only the council can make it real.

Funds

Move a series' funds. The council is the only authority that can move money, and the default spending limit for agents is zero without council approval, unless the council sets a written threshold. A steward's signature on a movement of funds is a personal act with a record attached to it: who approved it, for what, and on what basis.

Budgets and contracts

Decide budgets, major contracts, and hiring at each quarterly review, and recommend pivot or kill where the studio model gives the council that role. A budget is a plan for spending other people's money, and the council's job is to ask whether the plan is honest, whether the numbers are real, and whether the spending serves the mission the series was chartered for.

Stage gates

Decide whether a project passes from one stage to the next — from idea to validation, validation to build, build to launch, launch to operation, and operation to exit or wind-down. Killing a project is treated in the design as a studio success, not a failure to hide. A council that cannot kill anything cannot govern anything.

Stewards also decide what not to do: declining a proposal, refusing a charter change, and withholding funds from a project that has not met its gate — even when the project is popular and the commercial head of the family would prefer it to proceed. Every studio project passes a Foundation mission-fit review before it receives a series number, and the Foundation board can withdraw mission approval from a project that fails the standard. A steward should expect to say no.

The proposal flow: draft, decide, execute

Every substantive act in the family design passes through the same three-step flow. Agents work in step one. Humans own steps two and three. The human gate is not optional, and it is not a formality — a project that fails a gate is paused or killed, not carried forward on agent momentum.

Inside that flow, a steward's work is mostly reading, asking, and voting. The eight agent roles in the design — research synthesis, task routing, proposal drafting, coordination, progress tracking, monitoring, collaborator scouting, and grant drafting — all produce drafts for a human to decide on. A steward reads the draft, checks it against the charter and the mission standards, asks what is missing, and then votes. The vote is signed, recorded in the minutes, and attached to the steward's name. That record is the accountability — and it is heavier than an advisory role, because it is not an advisory role.

How seats are filled — and how they are not

Seats are human, decided by the board. That is the rule. No one self-appoints. No one is seated by a token vote, a community poll, an agent's recommendation, or a donation. No one is seated by buying in, by contributing code, or by partnering with the Foundation. A person who wants a seat writes to the Foundation, the board considers the expression of interest, and the board decides — in writing, in its minutes, with the person's consent recorded. Until that happens, there is no seat, no authority, and no title.

That also means no seat is claimed filled on this page, and none should be read into it. The files name no confirmed Steward Council members, and this page names none. The Foundation's draft bylaws name Mike Rice as an initial director and co-founder, and name Russell Vacanti as co-founder — with his consent pending, meaning Russell Vacanti must confirm in writing that he accepts the role before the bylaws are adopted with his name in them. Consent pending is not a formality and it is not a presumption: until Russell Vacanti confirms in writing, his role is named but not accepted, and this page states exactly that and nothing more. The draft bylaws also record that a third director is still to be recruited, and that the board as drafted would consist of three or five directors, serving two-year terms. Directors serve without compensation, except reasonable documented reimbursement. The corporation as drafted would have no members: all corporate powers are exercised by the board.

What the board looks for in a steward is judgement, independence, and availability — in that order. Judgement, because a steward votes on charters, budgets, and the movement of funds, and a bad vote has consequences that cannot be undone by an apology. Independence, because a steward must be able to refuse a project the commercial head wants and recuse themselves from any decision in which they have a financial interest — disclosing it in writing before discussion and letting a majority of disinterested directors decide. Availability, because a council that cannot meet, cannot review, and cannot sign is not a council at all. The files do not list a fixed set of qualifications beyond that, and this page does not invent one.

Stewards hold no equity, no tokens, and no profit interests in the series they govern, and agents hold none either. A steward's authority is a responsibility, not an ownership interest, and a nonprofit co-founder holds no shares, no ownership, and no right to profits. A steward is also bound by the conflict rules: no loan from a nonprofit to a director or related entity except as California law expressly permits and disinterested directors approve; no sale or transfer of nonprofit assets below fair market value; no commingling of accounts; and no nonprofit guarantee of a SAFE, loan, or obligation of another entity. The governance tracker records one row per meeting or consent, and a filing is not treated as done until proof of it is filed in the entity's pack.

How to express interest

Write to x@agifuturefoundation.org with the subject line starting [Steward]. Say who you are, what governance or fiduciary experience you have, and why you want this seat rather than an advisory role — a steward is accountable in a way an advisor is not, and the board will want to know you understand the difference. Say which kind of seat you mean, if you have a view: a series council seat, or the Foundation's board itself, which is a different body with different duties. Disclose any financial interest you hold in the Foundation, in AGI Corp, in any series, or in any organisation that does business with them, because that disclosure will be required before any vote in any case, and making it early saves everyone time. Do not send credentials, keys, or anyone else's private information. A human replies — there is no automatic approval, no instant onboarding, and no guaranteed timeline. A reply may be a conversation rather than a decision, and a decision, if it comes, will be the board's, recorded in its minutes, after your written consent.
